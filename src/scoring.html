Method

How Our Practice Score Estimate Works

Every study app shows you a number. Most will not tell you where it came from. Here is ours, including the parts that are our judgement rather than the test maker's.

The Short Version

  • The range is fitted to the questions you have actually answered, using each one at the difficulty it was answered at.
  • We also track a rating for each skill, which moves after every question by how surprising your answer was. Those ratings decide what to practise next. They do not set the range.
  • The width of the range is the statistical uncertainty in the fitted estimate.
  • The range narrows as you practise, because the estimate becomes more certain. That is arithmetic, not a claim that you improved.
  • It is not a predicted score and it is not a guarantee. Only GMAC and College Board can give you a real one.

Where the Estimate Comes From

The probability of a correct answer is modelled as

P(correct) = 1 / (1 + e-(ability - difficulty))

That is the Rasch model, the one-parameter model used in item response theory. To turn your answers into an ability we fit that model directly: we search for the ability value that makes the answers you actually gave most likely, counting every question in the section at its own difficulty. A weak prior keeps the answer finite for somebody who has got everything right or everything wrong so far, and is outweighed by the evidence within about twenty questions.

Skill ratings are kept separately, on the Elo update rule: after each question a rating moves by the difference between what happened and what the model expected. Pelanek, in Applications of the Elo rating system in adaptive educational systems (Computers and Education, 2016), sets out that the two approaches "share the same functional form of the model for predicting the probability of a correct answer, they differ only in the parameter estimation procedure." The difference matters here, and it is worth being plain about why. Elo is an online rule tuned to move gradually, which is what you want for deciding the next question but not for a number somebody reads after forty questions: spread across a dozen or more skills, a rating has barely moved by then. We used to average those ratings into the range, and the result sat close to the middle of the scale for everybody. Fitting the model to the answers instead needs no settling-in time, so the first range we show you is built on the same evidence as the hundredth.

Why Multiple Choice Changes the Math

On a multiple-choice test some correct answers are luck. The model accounts for it with a guessing floor: the chance of a correct answer never drops below one over the number of choices. A GMAT Focus item has five choices and an SAT item has four, so the same performance means slightly different things on the two exams, and we score them differently rather than pretending they are the same.

This also decides how fast the range tightens. An item tells you most when its difficulty sits near your ability, and least when you were always going to get it right. Practising at the edge of what you can do narrows the range faster than drilling questions you have already mastered.

How the Next Question Is Chosen

Most of every round goes to your three lowest-rated skills. Once a skill has six answers behind it, its questions are chosen at a difficulty you should get right about seven times in ten: hard enough to teach you something, easy enough that the answer still tells us where you are. Before that, the difficulty follows your ability across the whole section. A missed question comes back a day later, then at growing intervals each time you get it right.

Weakest-first has one failure we measured rather than assumed. A student who is struggling everywhere keeps the same few skills at the bottom, so the rest stop being asked at all, and the dashboard can never say anything about them. In simulations using the engine's own model, a student at about 35 percent accuracy on the fifteen-skill ACT got, on average, less than one question on their least-practised skill in a 140-question sitting, and some skills got none. So there is a floor: any skill with fewer than half an even share of your questions gets one before targeting fills the round, and the floor never takes more than three questions in ten. In the same simulations the least-practised skill rose to at least four questions at every ability level we tested. The cost is small and we measured it: with one section planted as a weakness, the ACT diagnosis found it in 57 of 60 simulated sittings, against 59 before.

Why the Range Has a Floor

The half-width of the range is the standard error of the ability estimate, converted onto the exam's scale. We then refuse to show anything tighter than 30 points, however much you practise. A tighter band would imply a precision that our question bank, which is not the real exam, cannot support.

We also show nothing at all until you have answered enough questions. A score built on a handful of answers is noise wearing a number's clothes, and a confidently wrong number is worse than an honest blank.

What It Cannot Do

  • It cannot account for test-day nerves, timing pressure across a full sitting, or the specific form you are given.
  • It cannot see anything you practised somewhere else.
  • It is built on our questions, which are written to the published specifications of each exam but are not the exam.
  • Calibrate against an official GMAT practice exam or an official Bluebook practice test from College Board. Where our estimate and an official practice test disagree, believe the official one.

Frequently Asked

Is This a Predicted Score?

No. It is an estimate of your current ability expressed on a familiar scale, so you have something to aim with. Test day is a different thing.

Why Did My Range Move Down After a Good Session?

The range reflects difficulty, not just accuracy. A run of correct answers on easy questions carries little information and can leave the estimate roughly where it was, while the added evidence tightens the band. Accuracy alone is a worse signal than accuracy against difficulty.

Do Flashcards Count?

Yes, at a quarter weight. Recognising a term is weaker evidence than answering a full question under time, and counting it equally would tighten the range on evidence that does not deserve it.
