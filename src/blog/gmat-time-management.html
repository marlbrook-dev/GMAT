GMAT time management comes down to three habits: know your per question budget, check the clock at fixed checkpoints instead of after every question, and bail on any question that passes the two minute mark without a clear path forward. On the GMAT Focus Edition, every section runs 45 minutes: 21 questions in Quantitative Reasoning, 23 in Verbal Reasoning, and 20 in Data Insights. This guide gives you a checkpoint table for all three sections, plus a plan for the bookmark and answer change tools.

How much time do you get per question?

The GMAT Focus Edition is 2 hours 15 minutes of testing: three 45 minute sections, one optional 10 minute break, and your choice of section order (mba.com, 2026). The arithmetic sets your budgets. Quant gives you 21 questions in 45 minutes, about 2 minutes 9 seconds each. Verbal gives you 23 questions, about 1 minute 57 seconds each. Data Insights gives you 20 questions, an average of 2 minutes 15 seconds, though that average hides wide variance: a multi source analysis set can fairly take 3 minutes, while a lean data sufficiency item should finish well under 2.

All three sections are scored from 60 to 90 and count equally toward the 205 to 805 Focus Edition total (mba.com, 2026), so there is no section where sloppy pacing is cheap. Much of what makes this exam feel difficult is the clock rather than the content, a distinction we take apart in our guide to how hard the GMAT really is.

What are the checkpoint times for each section?

Do not check the timer after every question. That habit burns attention and feeds panic. Instead, memorize four checkpoints per section and glance at the clock only near each one. The table below shows where you should be as the clock counts down from 45:00.

Read the table as a tolerance band, not a tripwire. One question behind at a checkpoint is noise; hold your normal pace and the section will absorb it. Two or more questions behind is a signal: on the next question that looks expensive, commit to your best guess early and move on. If you are ahead of a checkpoint, resist the urge to slow down and second guess finished work. Bank the time instead; a hard question late in the section will spend it for you.

What is the bail rule?

The rule is simple. When a question crosses the two minute mark and you still cannot name the specific step that gets you to an answer, stop. Pick the best remaining choice, bookmark the question, and move to the next one. Not work faster. Stop.

The math behind the rule is unforgiving. A five minute struggle on one question forces rushed guesses on two or three later ones, and those later questions may be ones you could have answered cleanly. Trading one question to protect three is a good deal every time it appears. The rule has an older cousin in data sufficiency, where the format rewards deciding rather than solving; our two minute rule for data sufficiency walks through that logic with examples. Data sufficiency now lives inside the Data Insights section (mba.com, 2026), so the same discipline pays there.

One caution for the end of any section: if the clock expires before you reach the final question, whatever you never saw earns nothing. A guess can be right and an unseen question cannot. In the last minutes, favor submitted answers over perfect ones.

How should you use the bookmark and three answer changes?

The Focus Edition lets you bookmark questions as you go, review the section if time remains, and change up to 3 answers per section (mba.com, 2026). These tools reward a plan and punish improvisation.

  • Bookmark selectively. Mark only bailed questions and genuine coin flips. A section with eight bookmarks has no plan. A section with two or three tells you exactly where any spare minutes should go.
  • Review only with real time. Reopening a question with 40 seconds left rarely helps. If you finish with 3 or more minutes, return to your bookmarks in order of confidence, least confident first.
  • Change an answer only for a reason you can say out loud. I misread the question, or I dropped a negative sign: those justify a change. A vague feeling that the answer looks wrong does not. You get 3 changes per section; doubt alone should not spend them.

How do you drill pacing without burning official questions?

Official practice questions are a finite resource, and a question you have already seen can never again measure your accuracy. Protect them. Save fresh official material for full 45 minute sections and complete mocks, where realistic difficulty matters most. Pacing itself is a separate skill, clock behavior rather than content knowledge, and you can train it on almost any question source.

  • Run micro sets. Five questions in a scaled time box: 10 minutes 45 seconds for Quant work, 9 minutes 45 seconds for Verbal, 11 minutes 15 seconds for Data Insights. Small sets give you many low cost repetitions of checkpoint checking.
  • Review the tail, not the average. An average of 2:05 per question can hide two 4 minute disasters. Log your time on every question and study everything over 3 minutes. Ask what should have told you, at the 90 second mark, that the question was a bail.
  • Rehearse the bail itself. Seed a practice set with one question above your level and run the full motion: recognize the wall, guess, bookmark, move on. The skill is emotional as much as tactical, and it will not show up on test day unrehearsed.
  • Reuse old questions for pure pacing. Familiar content no longer tests accuracy, but it still lets you rehearse checkpoints and transitions at full speed.

Memorize your checkpoints before test day. Working them out under the clock is itself a pacing error. A pacing plan is not a promise to be fast; it is a promise that no single question will tax the ones that come after it.
