Read for structure, not for detail. That is the whole GMAT Reading Comprehension strategy: map what each paragraph does, pin down the author's main point, and go back to the passage whenever a question asks about specifics. The math forces this approach. Verbal Reasoning gives you 45 minutes for 23 questions (mba.com, GMAC, 2026), just under 2 minutes each, and every fact you try to memorize on the first read spends seconds the questions will demand back.

What is a passage map?

A passage map is a short outline that records the job of each paragraph and the author's overall purpose, not the content details. One line per paragraph is enough: this one introduces a puzzle, this one presents the standard explanation, this one raises an objection and takes a side. Add a single verb for the author's purpose, something like describe, argue, evaluate, or reconcile, and the map is complete.

Keep it small on purpose. A useful map runs 15 to 25 words, jotted in fragments or simply held in your head. The moment a map starts collecting names, dates, and mechanisms, it has turned back into the detail retention it was meant to replace. The one exception worth flagging as you read: opinion language. Words like unfortunately, remarkably, or overstated mark where the author stops reporting and starts judging, and tone questions live exactly there.

What is the main-point-first read?

The first read has a single goal: finish able to state the author's main point in one sentence of your own words. Read every sentence once at a steady pace, but sort what you meet into two piles. Structure signals get full attention: however, therefore, critics contend, a second explanation. Technical content gets a location tag only. You note that paragraph two lists three objections. You do not learn the objections.

That is what read less, answer more means in practice. You are not skipping text, you are refusing to process it at a depth the questions will not pay for. If you reach the end and cannot say the main point, take twenty more seconds and settle it before opening a question. Questions that hinge on the big picture arrive with no line reference, so the main point is the one thing you genuinely must carry with you.

Where does the answer live for each question type?

Every common Reading Comprehension question type draws its answer from one of three places: your passage map, specific lines you go back to, or a small inference built from stated ideas. None of them draws on memorized detail.

The GMAT rewards the conclusion that must follow from what is written, not the one that plausibly could. How should you pace Reading Comprehension in a 45 minute section?

Start from the section's fixed shape: 45 minutes, 23 questions, an average of just under 2 minutes each (mba.com, GMAC, 2026). With Sentence Correction gone from the Focus Edition, those questions are Reading Comprehension and Critical Reasoning only, so RC pacing and CR pacing draw on the same clock.

A workable budget inside that average: 2 to 3 minutes to read and map a passage, then 60 to 90 seconds per question, targeted rereads included. The check that keeps you honest is per passage, not per question: a passage's total time, reading included, should land near 2 minutes for each question it carries. A three-question passage that consumes nine minutes has overdrawn the account, and the debt gets paid at the end of the section, where rushed questions go.

The map earns its keep here. Targeted rereads cost 15 to 30 seconds because the map tells you which paragraph to open. A detail-heavy first read costs a minute or more up front, much of it spent on material no question ever asks about. When a question resists two reads of the passage, use the section's own tools: answer, bookmark, and move, since the exam lets you return and change up to 3 answers per section (mba.com, GMAC, 2026). Why does going back beat memorizing?

Because wrong answers are built for people who half remember. A typical trap choice recycles real words from the passage and quietly changes the relationship between them: a cause becomes an effect, a critic's view becomes the author's, a qualified claim loses its qualifier. Familiarity is the bait. If you answer from memory, the choice that sounds most like the passage wins, and it is wrong.

Going back converts a memory test into a matching test. You reread the two or three relevant lines, hold them on screen, and check each answer choice against the actual wording. The distortions that fool a half memory are obvious next to the source. Thirty seconds of rereading routinely beats three minutes of deliberating from recall.

Going back also pays retroactively, on the first read. Knowing you will return removes the pressure to retain, which is what lets the first read stay fast and structural. Readers who try to memorize read slowly and still go back. Readers who map read quickly and go back on purpose. The passage never leaves the screen, so retention was never the skill. Knowing where to look is.

The bottom line

Map the passage: one line per paragraph plus the author's purpose. Read once for the main point, tagging where details live rather than learning them. Answer big-picture questions from the map, and go back to the text for details and inferences, checking answer choices against actual wording. Hold each passage near 2 minutes per question inside the 45 minute, 23 question Verbal section. Read less on the first pass, and you will have the seconds to answer more.
