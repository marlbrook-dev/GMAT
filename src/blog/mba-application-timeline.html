Start your MBA application timeline about 12 months before your first deadline. Round 1 deadlines cluster in early fall and Round 2 deadlines in early January, so the plan works backward from there: finish the GMAT by roughly four months out, brief your recommenders three months out, draft essays over the final two months, and hold the last month for application forms and proofreading. This guide turns that backward logic into a month by month schedule for both rounds.

How do MBA application rounds work?

An application round is a fixed submission window with its own deadline and its own decision date. Most full-time MBA programs run three rounds a year: Round 1 deadlines typically land in early fall, Round 2 deadlines in early January, and Round 3 deadlines in spring. Exact dates differ by school and can move from year to year, which is why the last section of this guide is about verifying them at the source rather than trusting any list, including this one.

The first two rounds do most of the work. Round 1 suits applicants whose materials can be genuinely ready by early fall, and it gives reapplicants and candidates from heavily represented industries the largest number of open seats. Round 2 offers roughly three extra months and remains a full-strength option at most programs. Round 3 is thinner, with fewer remaining seats and a compressed decision window. Plan for Round 1 or Round 2 and keep Round 3 as a fallback rather than a target.

Why does test prep come first?

Because the test is the slowest and least predictable piece of the application. Essays respond to a few focused weeks. Recommendations take coordination but not months of your own labor. A GMAT score is different: meaningful gains usually take two to three months of structured study, and many applicants need a second sitting. If the test is still unfinished when essay season arrives, it drains attention from every other part of the file at once.

So work the numbers backward. A structured prep window runs 6 to 12 weeks for most people, a schedule we lay out in our guide to how long to study for the GMAT. Add a buffer for a possible retake and the conclusion is plain: aim to be done testing by late spring for Round 1, or by early fall for Round 2. Set the score target before you begin. Reported numbers at the most selective programs sit high on the GMAT Focus Edition scale; Harvard Business School, for example, reports a median GMAT Focus score of 685 (HBS Class of 2027 profile, 2025). What a sensible target looks like for your own list is the subject of our guide to what counts as a good GMAT score in 2026.

Two rules keep this schedule honest. First, testing and essays never overlap. The retake window closes before drafting begins, and if a score is still short at that point, the right move is usually to switch rounds rather than to run both projects at once. Second, recommenders get a full quarter of lead time. Ask three months out, hand over your goals, your resume, and a short list of stories worth telling, then check in monthly. Busy managers write better letters with twelve weeks of notice than with two.

The essay block deserves sequencing of its own. Spend the first weeks collecting material rather than writing: the projects you led, the decision you would take back, the reason this degree and this school. Then draft one application completely before touching the next. A finished first application becomes a quarry for the others, while four half-drafted essay sets stay half drafted for weeks.

What changes if you target Round 2?

The order changes not at all; the calendar simply shifts by roughly one season, and two new pressure points appear. The first is December. The final month before an early January deadline is, for most applicants, the most crowded month of the personal calendar. Treat the start of December as your real essay deadline and reserve the holidays for forms, proofreading, and rest. The second is the temptation to keep retesting with the extra time. Set a final sitting date in early fall and hold to it, because a score that needs one more month tends to keep needing one more month.

Round 2 is also the honest landing spot for a Round 1 plan that slips. If testing runs past late spring, converting to Round 2 costs three months and usually buys a stronger file. Admissions teams read a rushed application easily; a finished Round 2 submission nearly always serves you better than a thin Round 1 one.

Where should you verify actual deadlines?

At the source, every time. Deadlines vary by school, shift from year to year, and are occasionally updated midcycle, so no third-party list stays reliable for long. Every program publishes its rounds on its official admissions site: see, for example, the admission pages of Stanford GSB and MIT Sloan. Once your school list is fixed, pull each program's dates from its own page, put them in a single sheet alongside recommender status and essay progress, and set your personal deadline several days ahead of the official one. Portals slow down and recommenders travel; the buffer costs nothing until the day it saves you.

The bottom line

Give the whole project about 12 months, and give the test the first of them. Finish the GMAT by late spring for Round 1 or by early fall for Round 2, hand your recommenders a full quarter of lead time, spend the last two months on essays, and keep the final month clear for forms and proofreading. The plan itself is not complicated. It fails only when it starts late, and it usually starts late because the test ran long. Slot the test first, and the rest of the calendar follows.
