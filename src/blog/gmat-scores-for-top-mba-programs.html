Top MBA programs publish GMAT Focus figures in a narrow band near the top of the scale. Stanford GSB reports an average GMAT Focus score of 689, Harvard Business School a median of 685, Wharton an average of 676, and MIT Sloan a median of 675 (each school's Class of 2027 profile, 2025; Wharton via Poets & Quants). On the Classic scale the same four schools report 738, 730, 735, and 720. None of these numbers is a cutoff. Here is how to read them and how to set your own target from them.

Why is a published average not a cutoff?

Because enrolled classes spread widely around every headline figure. Stanford GSB pairs its 689 Focus average with a published range of 615 to 785, MIT Sloan pairs its 675 Focus median with a middle 80% of 645 to 735, and Wharton pairs its 676 Focus average with a range of approximately 620 to 725 (Class of 2027 profiles, 2025; Wharton via Poets & Quants). Students enrolled below the headline number at each of these schools.

The arithmetic of a median makes the same point. A median of 685 (HBS Class of 2027 profile, 2025) means about half of the school's GMAT Focus submitters scored 685 or below. Treat a published figure as the center of gravity of an enrolled class, not as a bar you must clear before an application is worth sending.

How should you think in percentiles?

Whenever two editions appear side by side, convert to percentiles before comparing. GMAC's official concordance tables put a Classic 700 and a Focus 655 at the same 90.5th percentile, with a Classic 710 near a Focus 665 and a Classic 740 near a Focus 695 (GMAC official score concordance tables); the exam's official pages on mba.com carry the details. A Focus number roughly 45 points below a Classic number can represent the same rarity of performance. Our guide to the GMAT scoring scale walks through the anchors one by one. The working rule: read Focus figures against Focus figures, Classic against Classic, and percentiles across editions.

How do you set a target score from published figures?

  1. Pull the official numbers. Use each school's own class profile page, such as MIT Sloan or Wharton, and note the stat type, the edition, and the spread behind every figure.
  2. Anchor on your most selective target. Set your working target at or slightly above that school's published average or median; on the Focus scale, the four figures above run from 675 to 689 (Class of 2027 profiles, 2025).
  3. Use the published spread as your floor. A score inside a school's middle 80%, like MIT Sloan's 645 to 735 Focus band (MIT Sloan Class of 2027 profile, 2025), keeps you in the conversation; a score below the bottom of the spread makes that school a reach on numbers alone.
  4. Set one number and stop adjusting. A target that moves every week is a symptom of anxiety, not strategy. Fix it, build the study plan, and revisit only after a full practice test.

When is your score done?

Your score is done when it sits at or above the published average or median at your target schools, or comfortably inside their published spreads while the rest of your file is still unwritten. Past that point, extra points buy less than the same hours spent on essays, recommenders, and your resume. Committees read whole files, and a polished file with a score at the median is a stronger position than a rushed file with a score above it. When does a retake make sense?

Retake when three things line up: your score sits below the published spread at most of your targets, your practice results say the official sitting undersold you, and the calendar leaves room to prepare properly before deadlines. The published numbers are a map, not a wall. Set a target from the official figures, reach the band your schools report, and then move your energy to the parts of the application that only you can write.
