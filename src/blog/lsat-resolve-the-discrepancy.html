A resolve the discrepancy question gives you two facts that seem to clash and asks which answer, if true, would let both be true. The method is short: state the two facts, ask what would have to be the case for both to hold, and choose the answer that supplies it. LSAC lists ten skills its Logical Reasoning questions are designed to assess, and identifying explanations is one of them (LSAC, 2026).

What Is a Discrepancy Question?

Each Logical Reasoning question gives a short passage and then one question about it, or, rarely, two (LSAC, 2026). In a discrepancy question the passage reports two things that do not seem to fit, for example something done to bring about a result and an outcome that seems to defeat it. LSAC's own sample asks which choice, if true, most helps to resolve the apparent discrepancy in the information above, and its explanation says the discrepancy arises because the passage presents two pieces of information that are in conflict (LSAC sample questions, 2026). The same task can be put as which choice most helps to explain the outcome. You are not deciding which fact is right. You accept both and look for the circumstance in which both are true.

A Four Step Method

  1. State the two facts. Put each in a short sentence of your own, such as: the station added ticket machines to shorten its queues; the average wait got longer.
  2. Name the gap. Ask what would have to be true for both sentences to hold. Here, for example, something may have added customers, cut the station's capacity to serve them, or changed how the wait is measured.
  3. Test each choice against both facts. The right answer leaves the first fact standing and explains the second. A choice that speaks to only one of them resolves nothing.
  4. Sort the wrong answers by kind. They fall into a few recognizable kinds, set out below, and naming the kind is faster than arguing with each choice.

A Worked Example

To shorten the queues in its ticket hall, a busy railway station installed three ticket machines beside its staffed window. In the following month, the average wait to buy a ticket at the station was longer than before.

Which one of the following, if true, most helps to resolve the apparent discrepancy described above?

(A) Fewer passengers bought tickets at the station that month than in the month before.

(B) Before the machines arrived, the queue for the staffed window often stretched out of the ticket hall.

(C) When the machines were installed, the station cut the opening hours of its staffed window by half.

(D) The machines were made by the company that built the station's departure boards.

(E) Passengers who used the machines took less time to buy a ticket than passengers at the window.

The two facts are that the station added machines to shorten its queues and that the wait got longer. The gap could be filled by something that added customers, cut the capacity to serve them, or changed how the wait was measured. (C) fills it: if the staffed window now opens for half as many hours, the passengers who used it crowd into fewer hours or join the machine queue, so the average wait can rise even with three new machines. (A) and (E) make the longer wait harder to explain, since fewer customers and faster machines should both shorten it. (B) explains why the machines were installed, which is the first fact, and says nothing about the second. (D) is about who made the machines and bears on neither fact. The answer is (C).

Three Kinds of Wrong Answer

KindWhat it doesIn the example
Deepens the puzzleMakes the outcome harder to understand, for example by ruling out an explanation(A), (E)
Explains halfExplains why the first fact was expected or why a change was made, not the outcome(B)
No bearingStays on the topic and touches neither fact(D)

LSAC's explanation of its sample rejects one choice because, if true, it would rule out a possible resolution, and another because it introduces a third scenario that does not explain anything about either situation in the passage: the first and third kinds in the table.

A right answer can be a third factor: something else that changed at the same time, a group that behaved differently than expected, or a change in how the outcome was counted. In the example it is the shorter hours at the staffed window. In LSAC's sample the right answer is a group: only very careful drivers use headlights when they are not required to, so a law making headlights mandatory brings in drivers of all sorts.

When a Question Asks Which Choice Does Not Help

A question can turn the task around. One worded "Each of the following, if true, contributes to a resolution of the apparent discrepancy described above EXCEPT" asks for the one choice that does not help: four choices each resolve the discrepancy, and the answer is the one that does not. Run the same test on every choice. The four that make both facts true are the wrong answers, and the one left over deepens the puzzle or bears on neither fact.

Common Traps

  • Choosing an answer that explains only the first fact, such as why a change was made or why it was expected to work.
  • Arguing with a fact. LSAC's advice is to answer each question on the basis of the information given, even if you do not agree with it (LSAC, 2026), so an answer that only casts doubt on one fact resolves nothing.
  • Choosing a statement because it is true or on the topic. LSAC also warns against picking a response simply because it is a true statement, since it may not answer the question posed, and a wrong answer can stay on the same station, road or library and still bear on neither fact.

How Should You Practice?

Drill the first two steps until they take seconds: state the facts, name the gap.
