An LSAT method of reasoning question asks how an argument proceeds, and the right answer describes the argument's moves in general terms, with every part of the description true of the argument. Among the ten skills LSAC lists for its Logical Reasoning questions are recognizing the parts of an argument and their relationships, and reasoning by analogy, and LSAC's own sample of this question type turns on an analogy (LSAC, 2026).

What the Question Asks

LSAC's sample asks "The executive's reasoning does which one of the following?", and its explanation describes the task as identifying how the executive's reasoning proceeds (LSAC sample questions, 2026). Its answer never mentions the topic of the argument. It says the reasoning uses a case in which direct evidence is available to draw a conclusion about an analogous case in which direct evidence is unavailable.

A Four Step Method

  1. Find the conclusion and the evidence.
  2. Describe the move in general words. Does the argument reason from a comparison, an example, a counterexample, a sample, a cause, or by ruling out alternatives?
  3. Check every word of each choice. A description with several parts is only right if all of them are.
  4. Watch the kind of conclusion. A prediction, a general rule and a judgment about the past are different conclusions, and a choice that names the wrong one is wrong.

A Worked Example

Gardener: Last summer I watered half of my tomato plants every evening and the other half every morning, and the plants watered in the morning gave more fruit. So watering in the morning is better for tomatoes.

The gardener's reasoning does which one of the following?

(A) draws a general conclusion from a comparison between two groups treated differently

(B) relies on the judgment of an expert in the field

(C) predicts what will happen next summer from what happened last summer

(D) rejects a claim by showing that it leads to a contradiction

(E) compares two groups in order to show that the difference between them has no effect

The gardener treats two groups of plants differently, compares the results, and draws a general conclusion about tomatoes. (A) describes exactly that. (B) and (D) describe moves the gardener never makes: there is no expert and no contradiction. (C) names the wrong kind of conclusion; the gardener states a general rule, not a prediction about next summer. (E) is half right: the gardener does compare two groups, but concludes that the difference matters, not that it has no effect. The answer is (A).

Sorting the Wrong Answers

KindWhat is wrong with itIn the example
A move the argument never makesNothing in the argument matches it(B), (D)
The wrong kind of conclusionCalls a rule a prediction, or a judgment about the past a forecast(C)
Half rightMost of the description fits, but one part does not(E)

LSAC's explanation of its sample rejects choices of all three kinds. It rejects one because the executive does not infer a statistical generalization, and another because no distinction can be made in the argument between events of a general kind and a particular event. It rejects a third because the conclusion is not a prediction but a judgment about events that have already transpired, and a fourth on the same ground even though it correctly says the executive uses the comparability of two cases.

Common Traps

  • Picking a choice that is mostly right. One wrong word, such as calling a judgment about the past a prediction, makes the whole description wrong.
  • Matching words instead of moves. A choice that repeats the argument's vocabulary can still describe the wrong reasoning.
  • Describing the topic instead of the method. The right answer would fit any argument that reasons the same way.

How Should You Practice?

For each official question of this kind, write a general description of the argument's move before you read the choices, then cross out every choice with a single word that does not fit. Official questions are listed in our guide to free official practice tests, and the LSAT trainer drills the parts of arguments and how they fit together. Our guide to parallel reasoning questions uses the same habit of describing reasoning by its form.
