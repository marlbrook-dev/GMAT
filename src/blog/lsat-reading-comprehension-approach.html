An LSAT Reading Comprehension section has four sets, each a reading selection followed by five to eight questions, and LSAC suggests that reading each passage closely before answering may be the most effective approach under time conditions (LSAC, 2026). This guide sets out how the section is built and what LSAC advises about reading and answering.

How the Section Is Built

A selection is either a single passage or two related shorter passages, which LSAC calls comparative reading. A section includes three or four single passages and either one comparative reading pair or none (LSAC, 2026). The passages come from the humanities, the social sciences, the biological and physical sciences and areas related to the law, and LSAC describes them as generally densely written, with high-level vocabulary and sophisticated argument or complex rhetorical structure, such as multiple points of view.

Three Ways to Approach a Set

LSAC describes three approaches:

  1. Read the passage closely, then answer the questions.
  2. Read the questions first, then read the passage closely, then return to the questions.
  3. Skim the passage and questions quickly, then reread the passage closely and answer.

Because the LSAT is taken under time conditions, LSAC says the first approach may be most effective, and it suggests experimenting with all three to decide what works best for you. Practice is the place to run that experiment, timed, so the choice is settled before test day.

How to Read a Passage

  • Separate the main ideas from the supporting ones, and opinions and attitudes from factual information.
  • Follow the transitions. LSAC suggests circling words such as although, nevertheless and however, which show how the passage is put together.
  • Ask why the author makes a point and how the conclusions are drawn.
  • Mark what matters. LSAC suggests underlining the main idea and important arguments, and the descriptive words that show the author's attitude.

An unfamiliar subject is not a reason to worry. LSAC says you will not have to make any inferences based on prior knowledge, and that you may want to begin with the subjects that seem more familiar and leave the sets that seem difficult or unfamiliar for later.

How to Answer the Questions

  • Read every answer choice before choosing; the best answer is the one that most accurately answers the question posed.
  • Answer the specific question asked, and do not pick a choice simply because it is a true statement.
  • Answer only on the basis of the information in the passage.

What the Questions Ask About

LSAC lists what Reading Comprehension questions may ask about: the main idea or primary purpose, information that is explicitly stated, information or ideas that can be inferred, the meaning or purpose of words or phrases in context, the organization or structure, the application of information to a new context, principles that function in the passage, analogies to claims or arguments in it, the author's attitude as revealed in tone or language, and the impact of new information on its claims or arguments. Comparative reading questions concern the relationships between the two passages, such as generalization and instance, principle and application, or point and counterpoint.
