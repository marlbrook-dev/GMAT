LSAC's free Logical Reasoning sample set has ten questions, and each asks something different about a short argument, from where two speakers disagree to which argument shares a flawed pattern. The LSAT has two scored Logical Reasoning sections, beside one scored Reading Comprehension section and one unscored section of either type, each 35 minutes (LSAC, 2026). This guide sets out the ten, what LSAC says about each, and where to find our guide to answering it.

What LSAC Says Logical Reasoning Tests

LSAC lists ten skills its Logical Reasoning questions are designed to assess, among them recognizing the parts of an argument and their relationships, detecting assumptions made by particular arguments, identifying flaws in arguments and identifying explanations (LSAC, 2026). Each question gives a short passage and then one question about it, or, rarely, two, and no specialized knowledge of logical terminology is required.

What All Ten Have in Common

  • Read the question first for what it asks. A strengthen question and a flaw question can follow the same argument and need opposite answers.
  • Find the conclusion and the evidence. Almost every type turns on the gap between them.
  • Answer from the passage. LSAC's advice is to answer each question on the basis of the information given, even if you do not agree with it, and not to pick a response simply because it is a true statement.
  • Choose the best answer. LSAC's directions say more than one choice could conceivably answer some questions, and ask for the response that most accurately and completely answers the question.

How Should You Practice?

Work LSAC's ten samples first, since each comes with LSAC's own explanation of every choice, then move to full PrepTests, which our guide to free official practice tests lists. The LSAT trainer drills each Logical Reasoning skill between them, and our LSAT format and scoring guide covers the rest of the test.
