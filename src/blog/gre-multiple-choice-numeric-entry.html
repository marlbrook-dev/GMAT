Besides Quantitative Comparison, GRE Quantitative Reasoning asks questions in three formats: multiple choice with one answer from five choices, multiple choice with one or more answers from a list, and Numeric Entry, where you type the answer yourself. Each has its own way to lose a mark that the mathematics did not deserve, and ETS gives tips for each (ETS, 2026).

Select One Answer Choice

These questions ask you to select one answer from a list of five. ETS's tips:

  • Use the fact that the answer is there. If your answer is not among the five, assume it is wrong: reread the question, check your computations and reconsider your method.
  • Examine the choices. Sometimes a relationship between them points to the answer, and sometimes you can work backward by substituting each choice, though ETS warns that this can take longer than reasoning.
  • See how close an estimate needs to be. Scan the choices before you approximate: some questions need exact computation with only the final answer rounded, and for others an estimate is enough.

Example: If 3x + 7 = 25, what is x? (A) 4 (B) 5 (C) 6 (D) 7 (E) 8. Solving gives 6, and substituting it back, 3 times 6 plus 7 is 25, confirms it before you move on. The answer is (C).

Select One or More Answer Choices

These ask you to select one or more answers from a list, and the question may or may not say how many. ETS's tips:

  • Read what the question asks for. If it asks for a specific number of choices, select that many. If it asks for all that apply, consider every choice and select all of the correct ones and only those; there may be only one.
  • Find the bounds. When conditions limit the possible values, the least or greatest possible value can settle every choice at once.
  • Look for a pattern. Recognizing and continuing a numerical pattern saves a long calculation.

Example: Which of the following are factors of 36 that are greater than 5? Indicate all such numbers. (A) 4 (B) 6 (C) 9 (D) 12 (E) 18 (F) 24. Test every choice against both conditions. 4 divides 36 but is not greater than 5, and 24 is greater than 5 but does not divide 36, since 36 divided by 24 is 1.5. The answer is (B), (C), (D) and (E): all four, and only those four.

Numeric Entry

These ask you to enter the answer yourself: an integer or a decimal in a single box, or a fraction in two separate boxes, one for the numerator and one for the denominator. ETS's tips:

  • Answer the question that is asked. With no choices to guide you, read for the type of answer required, including any label beside the box, and watch units such as feet or miles, orders of magnitude such as millions or billions, and percents as against decimals.
  • Round only as instructed. If asked to round 46.7 to the nearest integer, enter 47. Compute exactly and round only the final answer, and if there are no rounding instructions, enter the exact answer.
  • Check that the answer is reasonable. Estimate, or solve another way, to make sure it fits the information given.

Example: A jacket's price is cut by 20 percent, and the new price is then cut by 25 percent. The final price is what percent of the original price? Enter your answer as a percent. Each cut multiplies the price: 0.8 times 0.75 is 0.6, so the answer is 60. Adding the two cuts to get 45 percent off, and entering 55, is the trap the question sets, and entering 0.6 answers in a decimal where the question asked for a percent.

How Should You Practice?

For select one or more, say each choice's verdict aloud before you submit; for Numeric Entry, reread the question's last line before you type.
