An LSAT parallel reasoning question asks which answer choice reasons in the same way as the argument it gives, and the answer is found by matching the form of the reasoning, not its subject. Recognizing similarities and differences between patterns of reasoning is one of ten skills LSAC lists for its Logical Reasoning questions (LSAC, 2026). Reduce the argument to letters, and the right answer is the choice that reduces to the same letters.

What a Parallel Question Asks

LSAC's sample asks "Which one of the following contains a pattern of flawed reasoning most similar to that in the argument above?" (LSAC sample questions, 2026). Its explanation sets out the task in two steps: understand the flawed pattern in the argument, then choose the response that exhibits the most similar flawed pattern. LSAC states the sample's pattern in letters: just because most As are Bs, that does not mean a particular B is likely to be an A.

A Four Step Method

  1. Reduce the argument to its form. Replace each term with a letter: every A is B; this is B; so this is A.
  2. Decide whether the form is valid or flawed. If the question asks for flawed reasoning, the answer must share the flaw, not merely be flawed.
  3. Reduce each choice the same way. Ignore the topic. A choice about trains can match an argument about paintings.
  4. Check strength and order. Every, most and some are different forms, and so are a conclusion drawn about a single case and one drawn about a whole group.

A Worked Example

Every member of the Larkspur choir can read music. Tom can read music, so Tom must be a member of the Larkspur choir.

Which one of the following contains a pattern of flawed reasoning most similar to that in the argument above?

(A) Every apple tree in the old abbey orchard was planted before 1950. This tree was planted before 1950, so it must be in the old abbey orchard.

(B) Every bus on the night route carries a first aid kit. This bus is on the night route, so it carries a first aid kit.

(C) Every runner in the club has run a marathon. Priya has never run a marathon, so she is not in the club.

(D) The houses on the street that were painted last spring sold quickly, so painting this house will make it sell quickly.

(E) Some members of the debating society study law, and some law students play chess, so some members of the debating society must play chess.

The argument's form is: every A is B; this is B; so this is A. That is flawed, since things other than choir members can read music. (A) has exactly that form and the same flaw: other trees were planted before 1950. (B) and (C) are not flawed at all: (B) applies the rule to a case it covers, and (C) correctly concludes that a case lacking B cannot be A. (D) and (E) are flawed, but differently: (D) takes something that went with a quick sale for its cause, and (E) assumes that the law students in the society are among the law students who play chess. The answer is (A).

Sorting the Wrong Answers

KindWhy it is wrongIn the example
Not flawedThe reasoning is good, so it cannot share a flaw(B), (C)
A different flawThe reasoning fails, but not in the same way(D), (E)

LSAC's explanation of its sample rejects choices of both kinds. It rejects three because the arguments are not flawed, since their premises, if true, provide good evidence for their conclusions, and one because it is flawed in generalizing from a specific case that may not be representative, which is not the flawed pattern in the passage's argument.

Common Traps

  • Matching the topic. A choice about the same subject as the argument is no more likely to be right than any other.
  • Picking any flawed choice. When the question asks for a similar flawed pattern, the flaw has to be the same one.
  • Missing a change in strength. Most is not every, and a choice that swaps one for the other has a different form.

How Should You Practice?

For each official parallel question you work, write the argument's form in letters before you read the choices, then write each choice's form beside it. Official questions are listed in our guide to free official practice tests, and the LSAT trainer drills parallel reasoning over arguments whose forms are proved valid or flawed.
