You can cancel a score on four of the five tests, and on the GMAT you never need to. GMAC says GMAT scores do not need to be canceled, because you see your official score before deciding whether to send it (mba.com, 2026). The GRE asks at the end of the test, before you see your scores (ETS, 2026). The LSAT gives you six calendar days after the test (LSAC, 2026), the SAT one week (College Board, 2026), and the ACT cancels on request (ACT, 2026).

GMAT: No Need to Cancel

GMAC's answer to "How do I cancel my score?" is that scores for the GMAT do not need to be canceled. Once your official score is in your mba.com account, you can review it and decide whether or not to send it to programs, and schools and programs have no access to your appointment details or official scores unless you choose to send them (mba.com, 2026). Official Score Reports go only to the programs you ask for, and if you take the exam again, a program sees your new score only when you send it. Up to five reports are free if you choose the programs within 48 hours of your official score becoming available, and our guide to free score reports covers what later ones cost.

GMAC can still cancel a score itself: its policies let it cancel or withhold scores when, in its judgment, there is a good faith basis to question their validity, and a candidate can appeal by submitting a request with supporting evidence (mba.com, 2026).

GRE: Decide Before You See Your Scores

At the end of the GRE General Test you choose to report (view) or cancel your scores, and once you view them you cannot cancel them. You cannot cancel one section and report the rest. Canceled scores are not available for you to review online, are not reported to any score recipient and are not added to your reportable history, and no refund is made, so testing again means registering and paying again (ETS, 2026).

A cancellation on the GRE can be undone: you can reinstate canceled scores online in your ETS account within 60 days after your test date, for a $50 fee (ETS, 2026). ETS also asks test takers to think carefully before canceling, because ScoreSelect lets you report only the scores you feel reflect your personal best. And canceling does not reset the retake rules: the GRE can be taken once every 21 days, up to five times in any rolling 12-month period, even if you canceled your scores on an earlier test (ETS, 2026).

LSAT: Six Calendar Days

If you did not buy LSAT Score Preview, you can cancel your score within six calendar days after your test date, on the LSAT Status page of JD Services or by contacting LSAC; on the deadline day, LSAC asks you to do it during business hours. Once your score is released it becomes a permanent part of your record, and you cannot cancel it unless you bought Score Preview (LSAC, 2026).

Score Preview lets you see your score first. It costs $46 if you sign up before the first day of testing for an administration and $87 after testing, and it is free with an approved LSAT fee waiver. You get your score on the release date with everyone else, then have six calendar days to keep or cancel it; if you do nothing, the score is added to your file and released to the law schools you have applied to (LSAC, 2026).

Either way, the choice is final. There are no refunds for canceled scores, and a valid cancellation request cannot be rescinded. A canceled score is recorded as a Candidate Cancel, and the score report law schools receive lists your cancellations and absences as well as your scores. A cancellation also counts against the annual and lifetime limits on repeating the test (LSAC, 2026), which our guide to retake limits sets out.

SAT: One Week After a Weekend Test

For the SAT on a weekend, you must cancel no later than 11:59 p.m. Eastern Time one week after your test date, and you can do it as soon as you leave the test center: sign in to My SAT, find the test date under My Scores, choose "Unsure about scores?" and agree to the Cancellation of SAT Scores Agreement. After you submit the request, the scores cannot be reinstated or reported to you or to any institution (College Board, 2026).

The SAT School Day works differently. A student who does not want the scores reported asks testing staff to submit a student-requested cancellation form in Test Day Toolkit within five days of the test day, and in some instances the scores may still be shared with schools, states or districts (College Board, 2026).

Canceling is not the only way to keep a score from a college. With Score Choice you choose, by test date, which scores to send, though some colleges and scholarship programs require all your scores, and Score Choice does not apply to the free score sends you pick when you register for a weekend test (College Board, 2026).

ACT: On Request

ACT's Terms and Conditions for Examinees say that unless you test under a State, District or On-Campus testing program, ACT will cancel your scores upon your request. If ACT has already sent them to official score recipients, it tells those recipients that the scores have been canceled, and it is not required to reinstate voluntarily canceled scores, even if you later change your mind (ACT, 2026). The terms name no deadline for the request.

That notice matters because ACT sends your scores automatically to the high school and colleges you chose when you registered. After that, you decide what else goes out: you can send the scores from a single test date, or your Superscore once you have tested more than once since September 2016 (ACT, 2026).

Cancel, or Just Don't Send?

On most of these tests, choosing what to send does what canceling would, without losing the score for good. GMAT scores go only where you send them, the GRE's ScoreSelect sends the scores you choose, SAT Score Choice sends the test dates you choose where a college allows it, and the ACT sends single test dates. The LSAT is the exception: the law schools you apply to receive every reportable result, cancellations included, so canceling is the only way to keep a score from them, and even then they see that you canceled (LSAC, 2026).

Canceling also rarely buys much. ETS and LSAC both say that canceling brings no refund, and neither the GRE nor the LSAT gives back a retake when you cancel (ETS, 2026; LSAC, 2026). So the better use of a disappointing test is usually the next one. The free trainers for the GMAT, GRE, LSAT, SAT and ACT run an adaptive round with no account needed, and report an estimated range from your practice, never a predicted score.
