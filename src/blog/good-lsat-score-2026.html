A good LSAT score is one above the middle of the distribution and inside the range your law schools publish, and LSAC's own percentile table sets the scale: a 160 is higher than 71.06 percent of test scores, a 165 higher than 85.17 percent, and a 170 higher than 94.48 percent (LSAC, 2026). The middle falls at 154. The table covers the 2023-2024, 2024-2025 and 2025-2026 testing years.

How Should You Read an LSAT Percentile?

LSAC defines the percentile rank for a score as the percentage of test scores that are lower than it, here over the 2023-2024, 2024-2025 and 2025-2026 testing years (LSAC, 2026). Two details follow from that wording. It counts test scores rather than people, so someone who tested twice appears twice. And because it counts only lower scores, even a perfect 180 sits at 99.85 rather than 100: the scores equal to yours are not counted as lower.

Where Do the Big Jumps Come?

Near the middle of the scale each point moves you past a large share of scores: from 154 to 155 the percentile rises from 50.43 to 54.03, about 3.6 points of percentile for one point of score. At the top the steps shrink: the 3 points from 170 to 173 add 3.11 points of percentile, less than the single point from 154 to 155 adds. Above 170, each point moves you past fewer scores than it does near the middle, which is one more reason to set the target from your schools rather than from the top of the scale.

What LSAT Score Should You Aim For?

Start from the schools. Look up the LSAT range each law school on your list publishes for its entering class, and aim at or above the middle of the highest one. The LSAT is scored 120 to 180 on the number of questions answered correctly, with nothing deducted for a wrong answer and every question weighted the same (LSAC, 2026), so every question you answer, even as a guess, can only help.
