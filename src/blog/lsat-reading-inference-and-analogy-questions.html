An LSAT reading inference question asks what the author would most likely believe or accept, given what the passage says, and an analogy question asks which new case works the way something in the passage works. Both go one step past the text, and the right answer is the step the passage supports, no further. LSAC lists information or ideas that can be inferred, the application of information in the selection to a new context, and analogies to claims or arguments in the selection among the things its Reading Comprehension questions may ask about (LSAC, 2026).

What the Questions Ask

LSAC's single-passage samples include one of each (LSAC sample questions, 2026). The inference question asks which proposal the passage provides evidence to suggest the author would be most likely to assent to, and LSAC's explanation says the task is not simply to identify something the author states explicitly but what can reasonably be inferred from what the author says. The analogy question asks which case is most analogous to someone who bought a tulip bulb at a very high price, only to sell a bulb of that variety at a much lower price, as an economist's account in the passage explains such sales. LSAC rates both difficult, by the number of test takers who answered each correctly.

How to Answer an Inference Question

  1. Gather the author's position. What the author favors, what the author objects to, and what the author takes for granted. In LSAC's sample the answer rests on two things: the tenor of the discussion makes clear the author believes a right should be protected, and the author regards a documentation requirement as an impediment to it.
  2. Ask for evidence, not just room. The right choice is one the passage gives you reason to think the author would accept. A choice the passage merely does not rule out is not enough.
  3. Check the size of the step. A choice that goes well past what the author argues is wrong however sympathetic the author seems.

How to Answer an Analogy Question

  1. State the relationship before the choices. LSAC's explanation advises getting clear on the specifics of the account first: the value of an original bulb reflects the earnings that can be made from its descendants.
  2. Test each choice for that relationship. In LSAC's sample the answer is a publisher who pays an extremely high price for a new novel only to sell copies at a price affordable to nearly everyone, because the copies stand to the novel as the descendants stand to the original bulb.
  3. Distrust a surface match. A choice with the same kind of thing, or the same rise and fall in price, can work in a different way.

A Worked Example

Historians have long blamed the ruin of Marrow Common on the dry summers of the 1790s. The parish's own grazing books suggest another cause. Until 1790 each household could put a fixed number of beasts on the common, its stint, and the stints together were kept below what the pasture could feed through a dry year. That spring the vestry abolished the stints, and any household could graze as many animals as it could keep through the winter. Within twenty years the common was bare.

The dry summers cannot explain this, for the commons at Tellford and Hartsell, which kept their stints, came through the same summers green. What ruined Marrow was not the weather but a common with no limit on any one household's share.

1. The passage provides evidence that the author would be most likely to accept which one of the following?

(A) A pasture shared by many households is more likely to survive dry years if each household's share is limited.

(B) All common land should be divided among the households that use it.

(C) Dry summers do no harm to pasture.

(D) The parish grazing books overstate how many animals the common carried.

(E) The vestry abolished the stints in order to ruin the common.

2. Which one of the following is most analogous to the stints as the author describes them?

(A) a fishery that allows each boat a fixed catch, set so that the stock can breed back each year

(B) a baker who limits each customer to two loaves during a shortage of flour

(C) a landlord who raises rents as more people want to live in a town

(D) a library that fines borrowers who return books late

(E) a village that deepens its well when the water runs low

For question 1, the author argues that the commons which kept a limit on each household's share survived the dry years and the one that dropped it did not, so the author would accept (A). (B) and (C) go much further than anything the author argues: the passage says nothing about dividing commons, and it says only that dry summers cannot explain the difference between Marrow and its neighbors, not that they do no harm. (D) cuts against the grazing books the author relies on, and (E) is never touched, since the passage gives no reason for the vestry's decision. The answer is (A).

For question 2, the stints work by giving each user a fixed share of a shared resource, set so that the resource can renew itself. The fishery's catch limits do the same: (A). The baker's limit looks similar, a fixed share for each customer, but it spreads a short stock of bread fairly, and nothing in it lets the flour renew. The landlord, the library and the well have no fixed share of a shared resource at all. The answer is (A).

LSAC's explanation of its inference sample rejects a choice that is a far cry from what the author thinks, one that conflicts with an intent the author never questions, one that cuts against what the author takes for granted, that the provincial courts are the right venue, and one for which there is no evidence, since the author calls the constitutional language necessarily general. Its explanation of the analogy sample rejects a choice with nothing akin to the relationship between an original bulb and its descendants, one that describes a loss rather than recouping the original value through an accumulation of smaller sales, and one that seems similar on the surface but is disanalogous in crucial respects.

Common Traps

  • Choosing what the author would like rather than what the author would accept. Sympathy with a cause is not support for every proposal made in its name.
  • Treating silence as support. A choice the passage never rules out still needs evidence that the author would accept it.
  • Matching the surface of an analogy. The same kind of object, or the same rise and fall in price, can hide a different relationship.

How Should You Practice?

Before the choices, write the author's position in a line for an inference question, or the relationship in general words for an analogy question. The LSAT trainer asks inference questions about every one of its long passages and includes analogy questions in its hand-written reading sets.
