An LSAT principle question asks for a general rule that, if valid, most helps to justify an argument's reasoning, and the right answer is the rule that carries the premises to the conclusion. Identifying and applying principles or rules is one of ten skills LSAC lists for its Logical Reasoning questions (LSAC, 2026). The work is the same as for an assumption question: find the gap, then find what closes it, this time stated as a general rule.

What a Principle Question Asks

LSAC's sample asks "Which one of the following principles, if valid, most helps to justify the journalist's reasoning?" (LSAC sample questions, 2026). Its explanation first notes that the journalist's premise does not, by itself, establish the conclusion, and then picks the principle that connects them. "If valid" means you accept the principle as true; you are not asked whether it is a good rule, only whether it would make the reasoning work.

A Four Step Method

  1. Find the conclusion and the premises. Note whether the conclusion is a judgment, such as that something should or should not be done.
  2. Name the gap. Ask what the premises leave unsaid that the conclusion needs.
  3. Say the missing rule in general terms. Replace the particular people and places with the kind of thing they are.
  4. Match it against the choices. The right principle covers this case and points the same way as the conclusion.

A Worked Example

Councillor: The council plans to close the Brook Street library branch because few people borrow books there. But the branch should stay open. Most of the people who use it come to read the newspapers and to use the computers, since they have no internet at home.

Which one of the following principles, if valid, most helps to justify the councillor's reasoning?

(A) A public service should be judged by everything people use it for, not only by the use it was first set up for.

(B) Public money should be spent where it does the most good.

(C) The computers in public buildings should be replaced every five years.

(D) A service that few people use should be closed so that its money can go to busier ones.

(E) Libraries should open in the evenings for people who work during the day.

The council judges the branch by how many books it lends; the councillor answers that people use it for other things. The gap is a rule saying those other uses count. (A) states that rule, so it is the answer. (B) states a value both sides could share: the council could say the money does more good elsewhere, so (B) gives the councillor's reasoning no support on its own. (D) supports the council, so it runs counter to the councillor's reasoning. (C) and (E) are about different issues, the age of the computers and the opening hours, and are not relevant. The answer is (A).

LSAC's explanation of its sample rejects choices of all three kinds. It says one principle actually runs counter to the journalist's reasoning, one gives the reasoning no support, and two are not relevant to it, one of them because it concerns a different issue from the one the journalist is addressing.

Common Traps

  • Picking a principle you agree with. "If valid" asks only whether the rule would make the reasoning work.
  • Picking a principle that supports the other side. A rule can be about exactly the right subject and still point the wrong way.
  • Picking a principle that is too general to decide the case, such as one about doing the most good, which either side could claim.

How Should You Practice?

For each official principle question you work, write the missing rule in general terms before you read the choices. LSAC's list names applying principles as well as identifying them, and our LSAT trainer drills both: finding the rule behind an argument, and deciding which case a given rule covers.
