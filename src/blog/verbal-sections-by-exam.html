All five tests read and reason, but they ask for different things. The GMAT's Verbal Reasoning section pairs reading with the evaluation of arguments (GMAC, 2026). The GRE gives about half of its verbal measure to completing sentences and paragraphs (ETS, 2026). The LSAT gives two of its three scored sections to Logical Reasoning (LSAC, 2026). The SAT and the ACT test grammar and rhetoric as well as reading, in domains their makers publish with the share of questions in each (College Board, 2026; ACT, 2026). Here is what each test's verbal side covers, in its maker's own words.

GMAT: Reading Comprehension and Critical Reasoning

The GMAT's Verbal Reasoning section consists of 23 multiple-choice Reading Comprehension and Critical Reasoning questions, to be completed in 45 minutes. GMAC says it measures the ability to read and comprehend written material and to reason and evaluate arguments (GMAC, 2026).

The two question types measure different things. Reading Comprehension questions measure the ability to understand words and statements, understand logical relationships between significant points, draw inferences, and follow the development of quantitative concepts. Critical Reasoning questions measure the ability to make arguments, evaluate arguments, and formulate or evaluate a plan of action (GMAC, 2026). GRE: Passages, and Sentences to Complete

GRE Verbal Reasoning is two sections, of 12 and 15 questions, in 18 and 23 minutes (ETS, 2026). ETS describes it in halves: about half of the measure requires you to read passages and answer questions on them, and the other half requires you to read, interpret and complete existing sentences, groups of sentences or paragraphs. The measure contains three types of questions, Reading Comprehension, Text Completion and Sentence Equivalence (ETS, 2026).

The reading half tests what ETS calls the abilities required to read and understand the kinds of prose commonly encountered in graduate school: understanding words, sentences and longer texts, distinguishing major from minor points, summarizing, drawing conclusions, reasoning from incomplete data, and identifying the author's assumptions and perspective (ETS, 2026).

LSAT: Two Sections of Arguments, One of Reading

The LSAT's multiple-choice portion is four 35-minute sections: two scored Logical Reasoning sections, one scored Reading Comprehension section and one unscored section of either type (LSAC, 2026). The unscored section can occur at any point in the test (LSAC, 2026), and the Reading Comprehension section is four sets, each a selection followed by five to eight questions (LSAC, 2026). Logical Reasoning is two of the three scored sections, and our guide to the LSAT format and scoring covers how they are scored.

SAT: Four Domains in Reading and Writing

The SAT's Reading and Writing section is 54 questions in two 32-minute modules, 64 minutes in all (College Board, 2026). Every question belongs to one of four content domains, and College Board publishes the share of each (College Board, 2026):

ACT: English and Reading, by Reporting Category

The ACT splits its verbal side in two: English, 50 questions in 35 minutes, and reading, 36 questions in 40 minutes (ACT, 2026). ACT reports English as Production of Writing (38 to 43 percent), Knowledge of Language (18 to 23 percent) and Conventions of Standard English (38 to 43 percent), and reading as Key Ideas and Details (44 to 52 percent), Craft and Structure (26 to 33 percent) and Integration of Knowledge and Ideas (19 to 26 percent) (ACT, 2026). Your score report shows how many questions you answered correctly in each category (ACT, 2026).

What the Differences Mean for Practice

The graduate tests weight argument differently. Critical Reasoning is one of the GMAT's two Verbal Reasoning question types, and Logical Reasoning is two of the LSAT's three scored sections, while the GRE spends about half of its verbal measure on completing sentences and paragraphs (GMAC, 2026; LSAC, 2026; ETS, 2026). Of the five, only the SAT and the ACT name grammar in their published content: Standard English Conventions on the SAT and Conventions of Standard English on the ACT (College Board, 2026; ACT, 2026).
