A class profile is a marketing document that happens to contain facts. Reading one well means knowing which numbers were chosen to impress you and which ones actually predict whether you belong in the applicant pool.

If you are applying from outside the United States, the order of importance is different from what a domestic applicant would use. Here is the order we would use.

First: Does the School Publish the International Share At All

The percentage of the class holding a non US passport tells you something no ranking does: whether the admissions committee reads applications like yours routinely or occasionally. A committee that reads several hundred foreign transcripts a year knows what a first class degree from an Indian university means, what a Japanese employer's title structure implies, and how to read a grading scale that is not a 4.0.

Of the 91 full time programs we track, 60 publish the figure and 20 report 40 percent or more. You can sort and filter by it directly in our MBA rankings, and the source and year are on every value.

A school that does not publish it is not necessarily closed to you. But when two programs otherwise look alike, the one that tells you is easier to plan around.

Second: The Spread, Not the Average

The average GMAT is the number schools lead with and the least useful one on the page. An average is the middle of a distribution that includes people well below it. What you want is the mid 80 percent range, which some schools publish and some do not: it shows you the actual band of admitted scores, and it is usually wider than applicants expect.

Two cautions specific to this moment. First, many schools now report both a GMAT Focus average, on the 205 to 805 scale, and a Classic average on the old 200 to 800 scale, because the class contains both. These are different scales and are never converted into each other; compare by percentile if you must compare at all, and check the current scale and percentile tables on the official GMAT scores page rather than a summary. Second, the percentage of the class that submitted each test matters. An average drawn from 14 percent of the class is a much weaker signal than one drawn from 70 percent.

Third: The Employment Report, Read for Its Denominator

A median salary with no reporting rate behind it is close to meaningless. What you want to know is how many graduates the figure covers. A program reporting a median of 150,000 dollars with 90 percent of the class reporting is telling you something. The same median with an unstated reporting base is telling you very little.

For an international applicant there is a second question the headline number hides: what share of graduates were hired by employers willing to sponsor. Very few schools publish it directly. It is worth asking the careers office, because it is the number that actually describes your outcome.

Fourth: Acceptance Rate, With a Caveat

Acceptance rate is a real signal of selectivity and a poor signal of your chances. It describes the whole pool, and the pool is not homogeneous. A 20 percent acceptance rate does not mean you have a one in five chance; it means one in five applicants was admitted, across every background, score, and region.

Note also how many schools do not publish it at all. Several of the most selective programs in the country do not, which means the schools with visible acceptance rates are not a representative sample.

Fifth: The Number That Is Not On the Page

None of these figures tell you whether the degree will let you work in the United States afterwards. That is decided by the program's CIP code, which no class profile prints, and which determines whether you get 12 months of post completion OPT or 36. It is covered in full in the CIP code piece. Ask for it in writing while you are still reading class profiles, not after you have decided.

Putting It Together

A reasonable shortlist process from abroad looks like this. Filter by international share to find programs used to reading applications like yours. Check the score spread rather than the average, and note which edition and what share of the class submitted. Read the employment report for its denominator. Ask each survivor for its CIP code. Then, and only then, look at where the rankings put them.
