An LSAT inference question asks which statement can be properly inferred from the passage, and the right answer is one the passage establishes, not one it merely makes plausible. Drawing well-supported conclusions is one of ten skills LSAC lists for its Logical Reasoning questions (LSAC, 2026). The work is to combine what the passage says and stop exactly where it stops.

What an Inference Question Asks

LSAC's sample asks "Which one of the following statements can be properly inferred from the passage?" (LSAC sample questions, 2026). The passage is not an argument to be judged. You take every statement in it as true and ask what follows from them. The right answer can combine two or more statements, so it may not be printed anywhere in the passage, but nothing in it may go further than the passage does.

A Four Step Method

  1. Read the passage as a set of facts. Note each claim, and note especially the strong ones: every, none, only, always.
  2. Link the claims. Ask what two of them say together that neither says alone.
  3. Test each choice against the passage. Can you point to the sentences that establish it? If not, it is out.
  4. Watch the strength of each choice. A choice that says only, always, never or because needs the passage to say as much.

A Worked Example

On the Marrowdene branch line, every train that leaves the city after eight in the evening stops at every station. The last train of the day on the line leaves the city at a quarter past ten at night. None of the trains that stop at every station has a buffet car.

Which one of the following statements can be properly inferred from the passage?

(A) The last train of the day on the line has no buffet car.

(B) Every train on the line that stops at every station leaves the city after eight in the evening.

(C) No train on the line that leaves the city before eight in the evening stops at every station.

(D) The last train of the day is the slowest train on the line.

(E) Passengers on the line prefer trains with a buffet car.

The last train leaves after eight, so it stops at every station, and no train that stops at every station has a buffet car. Two links give (A), so it is the answer. (B) turns the first rule around: the passage says late trains stop everywhere, not that only late trains do. (C) makes the same mistake from the other side, since the passage says nothing about earlier trains. (D) goes beyond the passage: stopping everywhere suggests a slow journey, but the passage compares no journey times. (E) brings in something the passage never mentions. The answer is (A).

LSAC's explanation of its sample rejects one choice because its conclusion goes far beyond what is established by the passage, and another because the passage offers no evidence that the way it names is the only way an outcome could have been prevented. It rejects a third because there is no evidence in the passage about the subject it raises. The first two are the first kind in the table, and the third is the last kind.

Common Traps

  • Picking the choice that is most likely to be true in real life. The passage is the only evidence that counts.
  • Picking a choice that reverses a rule. Every late train stopping everywhere does not make every train that stops everywhere a late one.
  • Rejecting the right answer because the passage never states it. A proper inference can link two statements and appear nowhere in the passage word for word.

How Should You Practice?

For each official inference question you work, point to the sentences that establish your answer before you mark it, and name the kind of every wrong answer.
