The GMAT scoring scale for 2026 is compact. The GMAT Focus Edition reports a total score from 205 to 805 in 10-point steps, so every total ends in 5. That total is built from three sections, Quantitative Reasoning, Verbal Reasoning, and Data Insights, each scored from 60 to 90 and weighted equally (mba.com, GMAC, 2026). There is no essay. Below is the full logic of the scale, the official conversion to the old 200 to 800 Classic scale, and the misreads worth avoiding.

How does the 205 to 805 scale work?

Start with the section scores. Each of the three sections returns a score between 60 and 90, and no section counts more than another: Quantitative Reasoning, Verbal Reasoning, and Data Insights contribute equally to the total (mba.com, GMAC, 2026). That is a real break from the Classic exam, where Integrated Reasoning and the essay sat outside the 200 to 800 total. On the Focus Edition there is no essay at all, and Data Insights, which absorbed Integrated Reasoning content along with Data Sufficiency, counts fully in the headline number.

The exam behind the chart is short. It runs 64 questions in 2 hours and 15 minutes: 21 Quantitative Reasoning questions, 23 Verbal Reasoning questions, and 20 Data Insights questions, with 45 minutes per section and one optional 10-minute break (mba.com, GMAC, 2026). You pick your own section order, and within each section you can bookmark questions and change up to 3 answers before time runs out.

Two features of the total scale matter whenever you read a chart. First, totals move in 10-point steps, so 655 and 665 are adjacent scores with nothing in between. Second, the endpoints are 205 and 805, not 200 and 800. A total ending in 0 cannot be a Focus Edition score. That detail sounds cosmetic, but it is the fastest way to tell which edition any quoted number belongs to.

How do Focus scores convert to Classic scores?

They convert by percentile, not by arithmetic. GMAC publishes official concordance tables that line the two editions up by percentile rank, and the anchor points are worth memorizing because they surprise most people. A Classic 700 and a Focus 655 sit at exactly the same point, the 90.5th percentile (GMAC concordance tables). Focus numbers run lower across the board, by roughly 45 points at the old 700 mark.

What are the most common misreads of the scale?

Comparing raw scores across editions

This is the big one. A Focus 655 looks 45 points weaker than a Classic 700, and it is not: the concordance places them at the same percentile. The error cuts the other way too. The tables put a Classic 740 near a Focus 695 (GMAC concordance tables), so a Focus total in the low 700s reflects rarer performance than the same digits did on the Classic scale. Percentile is the only honest basis for comparing the two.

Assuming the sections carry different weights

Classic-era habits die hard. For years, applicants treated Quant as the section that mattered and everything else as secondary. On the Focus Edition the three sections carry equal weight in the total (mba.com, GMAC, 2026), and Data Insights is a full third of the score, not a sidecar. A study plan that neglects Data Insights concedes a third of the scale before the first question.

Treating 805 like 800

An 805 is not an 800 plus five points. It is the ceiling of a different scale, earned on a different test. Reporting a Focus score without its edition label invites the wrong comparison, so label it in applications, in conversation, and in your own tracking. The same goes for section scores: a 90 on a Focus section lives on the 60 to 90 scale and has no direct Classic counterpart.

How do schools report scores on this scale?

Admissions pages now publish Focus figures alongside Classic ones, and they follow the same percentile logic. Harvard Business School, for example, reports a median GMAT Focus score of 685 for the Class of 2027 (HBS Class of 2027 profile, 2025). Other schools publish averages rather than medians, and the two are not interchangeable, so check which figure a school reports before you benchmark against it. Read the scale the way admissions offices do: edition first, percentile second, raw number last. On that reading, the 205 to 805 scale stops being confusing and becomes easy to work with. If the number ends in 5, it is a Focus score, and its meaning lives in its percentile.
