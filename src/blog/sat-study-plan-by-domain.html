College Board publishes how many questions each SAT content domain contributes to a section, and the counts are not equal: Algebra and Advanced Math supply 13 to 15 questions each on the Math section, while Geometry and Trigonometry supplies 5 to 7 (Digital SAT Suite Specifications Overview, Table 3). A study plan that ignores those weights spends the same hours on a domain worth a third as many points. Here is how to build one that does not.

Why Weakest First Is the Wrong Rule

The instinct is to attack whatever you scored lowest on. That rule ignores how much of the test the domain represents. Compare two students, each with ten hours:

StudentWeakest domainQuestions it carriesSecond weakestQuestions it carriesBetter use of the hours
AGeometry and Trigonometry5 to 7Algebra13 to 15Algebra, unless the geometry gap is far larger
BExpression of Ideas8 to 12Craft and Structure13 to 15Depends: the gap sizes are close enough to matter

The rule that replaces weakest first is simple. For each domain, multiply the questions it carries by the improvement you think is realistically available, and work on the largest product. A domain where you already score well has little room left, however many questions it carries; a domain worth six questions cannot repay ten hours no matter how far you have to climb.

How Do You Find Out Where You Stand?

Take a full-length practice test in Bluebook and read the score report, which breaks performance out by domain rather than giving you one number. That report is the input to everything above, and one test is enough to start; you do not need a diagnostic ritual before you begin studying.

Between tests you need the same signal more often, which means practicing in sets large enough to measure. Fifteen to twenty questions in one domain will tell you something; four will not. A one-sentence definition worth keeping: a domain gap is the difference between your accuracy in that domain and your accuracy across the whole section, and it is the number your plan should be shrinking.

What Should a Week Look Like?

A workable structure, assuming five to seven hours:

  • One timed module, alternating Reading and Writing with Math week to week. This is the measurement, so take it under real conditions.
  • Two sessions on your highest-product domain, in sets of fifteen to twenty questions, reviewing every miss by naming the specific error rather than the topic.
  • One session on Standard English Conventions if you are studying Reading and Writing. The rules are finite, the questions are the fastest points on the section, and this is the only domain where memorization reliably converts to score.
  • One short session revisiting misses from previous weeks. Questions you got wrong once are the highest-yield material you own.

The Pacing Consequence Most Plans Miss

Domain counts affect pacing as well as study time. A Reading and Writing module runs Craft and Structure first, then Information and Ideas, then Standard English Conventions, then Expression of Ideas, with similar skills grouped and arranged easiest to hardest inside each group. Math runs easiest to hardest across the module (Digital SAT Suite Specifications Overview).

That ordering is a clock you can read. If you are eighteen questions into a Reading and Writing module and behind, you know the conventions block is ahead and that those questions are usually quick. If you are grinding at Math question six, you are spending your best minutes on questions the test considers easy while the expensive ones are all still to come. Practicing in modules rather than loose sets is what makes that knowledge usable on test day.
