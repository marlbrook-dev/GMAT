An LSAT point of disagreement question gives you two speakers and asks what they disagree about, and the right answer is a claim one speaker accepts and the other rejects. Recognizing misunderstandings or points of disagreement is one of ten skills LSAC lists for its Logical Reasoning questions (LSAC, 2026). The test for each choice is short: what would each speaker say to it?

What the Question Asks

LSAC's sample gives two speakers and asks for the choice that completes "Laird and Kim disagree on whether pure research", and its explanation describes the task as identifying the point on which the two disagree (LSAC sample questions, 2026). Both speakers have to be committed: one to the claim and the other against it, on the evidence of what each actually says.

A Four Step Method

  1. Sum up each speaker. Put each position in a sentence of your own.
  2. Ask each speaker about each choice. Would the first speaker agree, disagree or say nothing? Then the second.
  3. Keep only a split. The answer is the choice where one speaker agrees and the other disagrees.
  4. Distrust silence. If one speaker never addresses a claim, you cannot say that speaker disagrees with it.

A Worked Example

Rana: The town should build its new swimming pool next to the secondary school, so that pupils can use it for lessons during the day.

Olu: The pool should not go next to the school, which is on the edge of town. It should go in the town centre, where most of the people who would swim there work, and they would use it before and after work.

Rana and Olu disagree about whether

(A) the town should build a new swimming pool

(B) the new pool should be built next to the secondary school

(C) pupils should have swimming lessons during the school day

(D) people who work in the town centre would use a pool there

(E) the pool should be open before and after working hours

Ask each speaker about each choice. Both take for granted that the pool will be built, so both accept (A). Rana says the pool should go next to the school and Olu says it should not, so (B) is a split, and it is the answer. Only Rana speaks about lessons, so (C) has no view from Olu. Only Olu speaks about people who work in town and about opening hours, so (D) and (E) have no view from Rana. The answer is (B).

Sorting the Wrong Answers

KindWhat is wrong with itIn the example
Both agreeA shared claim, such as the background both speakers build on(A)
Only one speaker addresses itThe other speaker's silence is not disagreement(C), (D), (E)

LSAC's explanation of its sample rejects choices of both kinds. It rejects three because the speakers agree on them, or would likely agree, and one that the first speaker clearly agrees with because nothing in what the second speaker says suggests disagreement with it; it adds that a speaker who calls one value the greatest has not said it is the only one.

Common Traps

  • Picking the claim one speaker makes most forcefully. The other speaker has to take the opposite view, not just ignore it.
  • Picking the background both speakers share. Two people arguing about where to build something both think it should be built.
  • Reading too much into a ranking. A speaker who says one thing matters most has not said that nothing else matters.

How Should You Practice?

For each official question of this kind, mark every choice with what each speaker would say to it, agree, disagree or nothing, before you choose.
