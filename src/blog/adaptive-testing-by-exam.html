The GMAT, the GRE and the SAT all adjust their questions to how you are doing, but at three different scales: the GMAT question by question, the GRE section by section, and the SAT module by module. That difference decides whether you can go back to a question, and when. Here is how each works, in each test maker's own words.

GMAT: Question by Question

GMAC describes a computer-adaptive test as one that adjusts the difficulty of questions based on your performance as you move through the exam. It typically starts with a medium-difficulty question; if you answer correctly, the next question may be more difficult, and if you answer incorrectly, the next question may be easier, and this continues throughout the section. Your score is based on the overall pattern of your responses, not just whether individual questions are right or wrong (GMAC, 2026).

That is why GMAC lists three factors in every section score: the number of questions answered, whether the answers are correct or incorrect, and the difficulty and other parameters of the questions answered (GMAC, 2026). You can still bookmark questions as you go and, once every question in a section is answered, change up to three answers on the Question Review and Edit screen, if time remains (GMAC, 2026).

GRE: Section by Section

The GRE's Verbal Reasoning and Quantitative Reasoning sections are section-level adaptive. The first section of each measure is of average difficulty, and the difficulty level of the second section depends on your overall performance on the first: ETS's own example is that if you do very well on the first Quantitative Reasoning section, the second will be at a higher level of difficulty (ETS, 2026).

Scoring follows the design. It takes into consideration the total number of questions answered correctly across the two sections, as well as the difficulty level of the sections, and ETS says its adaptive design allows you to move forward and backward throughout an entire section (ETS, 2026). SAT: Module by Module

Each SAT section is divided into two equal-length modules. The first module contains a broad mix of easy, medium, and hard questions, and based on how you perform on it, the second module will be either more difficult or less difficult. Reading and Writing is 54 questions in two 32-minute modules, and Math is 44 questions in two 35-minute modules (College Board, 2026).

Within a module you can move back and forth between questions and mark any for review until time expires, but once you move on from a module you cannot go back to it (College Board, 2026). Your scores depend on whether you answer questions right or wrong, the difficulty level of the questions, and the probability that you might be guessing (College Board, 2026). What Adapting Means for How You Take the Test

The three designs ask for slightly different habits. On the GMAT every answer shapes the next question, and GMAC's advice is to focus on answering each question to the best of your ability: trying to game the difficulty level will not improve your score, and consistent performance matters more than any single question (GMAC, 2026). On the GRE and the SAT, the first section or module sets the level of the second, so it deserves your full attention from the first question. And on the GMAT and in the SAT's first module, a question that feels hard is not a sign that you are doing badly: GMAC notes that you may see a mix of easier and harder questions, and the SAT's first module is a broad mix of easy, medium and hard questions by design (GMAC, 2026; College Board, 2026).
