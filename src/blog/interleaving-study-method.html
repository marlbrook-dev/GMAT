Interleaving means mixing different problem types within one practice session, in a pattern like ABC ABC ABC instead of AAA BBB CCC. Mixed practice beats topic blocks because a mixed set forces you to recognize each problem and choose a method before applying it, and that choice is what the exam actually tests. The GMAT Focus Edition asks 64 questions, 21 Quant, 23 Verbal, and 20 Data Insights (mba.com, 2026), and none of them is labeled by chapter.

What separates interleaved practice from blocked practice?

Blocked practice means solving many problems of the same type in a row: twenty rate problems, then twenty exponent problems, then twenty Critical Reasoning questions. It is the default almost everyone inherits, because textbooks are organized by chapter and chapter exercises block themselves. Interleaved practice shuffles those same problems together, so consecutive questions rarely share a method.

The research contrast between the two is well established and runs in a direction most students find uncomfortable. In studies of mathematics practice by Doug Rohrer and colleagues, students doing blocked work looked stronger during the practice itself, then lost that edge on delayed tests, where the interleaved groups came out ahead. Nate Kornell and Robert Bjork found the same shape outside of math: people shown painters' styles in mixed order got better at identifying new paintings by those artists than people shown one artist at a time, and most participants still believed blocking had served them better. That last detail matters. Interleaving does not merely beat blocking; it beats blocking while convincing you it is losing.

Why does mixed practice feel harder and store better?

Blocking lets you load a method once and reuse it. By the fifth rate problem in a row, you are no longer deciding how to solve anything. You are executing a procedure that is already sitting in working memory, and each repetition gets smoother. That smoothness is the fluency illusion at work: ease of execution in the moment gets mistaken for durable learning.

Interleaving takes that shortcut away. Every switch forces you to set one method down and retrieve the next from long-term memory, and effortful retrieval is precisely what strengthens a memory. Cognitive scientists in the tradition of Robert Bjork call this a desirable difficulty: a condition that makes practice feel worse while making retention better. Mixing also smuggles in a second advantage. When rate problems are spread through a week instead of packed into Monday, each return to the topic arrives after a delay, which is the same mechanism behind spaced repetition. An interleaved plan spaces itself.

What is discrimination learning, and why does it decide GMAT questions?

Discrimination learning means learning to tell similar things apart by the features that actually distinguish them. It is the quiet half of every GMAT skill. Knowing how to solve a weighted average problem is worth little if you cannot tell, in the first ten seconds, that a problem is a weighted average problem and not a simple ratio wearing a disguise.

Blocked practice removes that decision entirely. When the page says Rates, the hardest step of every problem on it has been answered for you before you start. Mixed practice restores the decision, and it does something subtler too: it puts confusable neighbors side by side. Solving a rate problem immediately after a work problem forces you to notice exactly where the two setups differ. The same logic runs through the verbal side. Strengthen and evaluate questions read alike until you have met them back to back. Data Sufficiency turns almost entirely on one discrimination, whether the question needs a specific value or only a relationship, and that judgment sharpens fastest when the two kinds alternate. Choosing the method is part of the skill, so practice that hides the choice trains a smaller skill than the exam measures.

How does Start From Nowhere interleave by design?

Start From Nowhere treats mixing as the default rather than a setting to configure. The trainer's rounds draw on 180 original practice items spanning Quant, Verbal, and Data Insights, so a round moves between skills instead of marching through a chapter. The selection is adaptive: every skill carries its own rating, keyed to the skill labels on the official GMAT Focus score report, and the mix leans toward the skills whose ratings lag. Misses return on a spaced schedule, and because a returning miss lands inside a later mixed round, your review arrives interleaved as well, surrounded by problems that do not resemble it. Timing is tracked per question, which matters here because hesitation at the start of a problem is often the method-selection step running slow. The first round is free and needs no account. The trainer supplies the mixing; the retrieval effort on each question is still yours, which is why it pairs naturally with active recall.

Is blocked practice ever the right choice?

Yes, briefly, at the start. When a method is genuinely new to you, a short block is how you learn its mechanics, because you need a few consecutive repetitions to build the procedure at all. There is nothing to discriminate before there is something to execute. The mistake is staying blocked after that point, practicing execution long past the moment when selection became the real bottleneck. A workable rule: block until you can solve the new problem type slowly but correctly without notes, then release it into the mix and never drill it in isolation again. From that day on, the mix does the teaching. It will feel rougher than the chapter drills you are used to. That roughness is not a malfunction. It is the sensation of practicing the entire skill, including the part the answer key cannot see: knowing what to do before you do it.
