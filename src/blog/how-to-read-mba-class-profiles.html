Read an MBA class profile in a fixed order: stat type first, score range second, headline number last. The stat type matters most because schools do not all report the same thing. Stanford GSB publishes an average GMAT Classic score of 738 (Stanford GSB Class of 2027 profile, 2025), while Harvard Business School publishes a median of 730 (HBS Class of 2027 profile, 2025). An average and a median answer different questions, so the gap between those two numbers means less than it appears to. This guide covers stat types, ranges, score editions, acceptance rates, and how to turn profiles into a shortlist.

Is the headline number an average or a median?

An average adds every enrolled student's score and divides by the class size. A median is the middle score: half the class scored at or above it and half at or below. Schools choose one or the other, and the four programs applicants benchmark against most often split evenly. Stanford GSB and Wharton publish averages, while MIT Sloan and Harvard Business School publish medians (each school's Class of 2027 profile, 2025).

The distinction matters because averages respond to outliers and medians do not. A handful of unusually high or low scores can move an average by several points while leaving the median untouched. Stanford's average of 738 and HBS's median of 730 on the Classic scale (Class of 2027 profiles, 2025) are two different kinds of summary, and ranking the schools by subtracting one from the other is a misread.

Check the edition label with the same care. Individual GMAT Focus Edition totals run 205 to 805 in 10-point steps, so every Focus score a person earns ends in 5 (mba.com, GMAC, 2026). A class statistic is different: Stanford's published Focus average of 689 (Stanford GSB Class of 2027 profile, 2025) ends in 9 because it averages many individual scores. A profile number that does not end in 5 can still be a Focus figure, even though it could never be a single Focus score.

What does a middle 80% range tell you?

A middle 80% range trims the top 10% and the bottom 10% of enrolled scores and reports the span that remains. It is the most useful line in a profile because it shows spread, not just center. MIT Sloan reports a median GMAT Focus of 675 with a middle 80% of 645 to 735 (MIT Sloan Class of 2027 profile, 2025); by the definition of the range, roughly one enrolled student in ten scored below 645. Wharton pairs its Classic average of 735 with a middle 80% of 680 to 770 (Wharton Class of 2027 profile; Poets & Quants, 2025), which tells the same story on the Classic scale.

A full range is wider still. Stanford publishes a Focus range of 615 to 785 next to its 689 average (Stanford GSB Class of 2027 profile, 2025). The floor of a full range can reflect a single admit, so treat it as proof that exceptions exist rather than as a target. The practical read: the center tells you what is typical, the middle 80% tells you what is common, and the full range tells you what is possible.

Why can Focus and Classic numbers never be compared directly?

Because the two editions are different tests on different scales. Classic totals live on the old 200 to 800 scale, while Focus totals run 205 to 805 (mba.com, GMAC, 2026). The only valid bridge between them is percentile rank. GMAC's official concordance tables place a Classic 700 and a Focus 655 at the same 90.5th percentile, with a Classic 710 near a Focus 665 and a Classic 740 near a Focus 695 (GMAC official score concordance tables). A Focus number that looks 45 points lower than a Classic number can describe identical performance.

So when a profile lists both editions, as MIT Sloan's does with a Classic median of 720 and a Focus median of 675 (MIT Sloan Class of 2027 profile, 2025), read each figure on its own scale. Never subtract across editions and never average them together. What do acceptance rates tell you, and what do they not?

An acceptance rate tells you one thing: how many offers a school extended relative to the applications it received in one cycle. That is a real signal of seat scarcity, and it is worth knowing before you anchor a plan on a single program. Schools publish the current figure in their official class profiles and admissions pages, which is where to check it.

What an acceptance rate does not tell you is your own probability. It blends every applicant pool into one number: strong and weak files, all industries, all regions, all rounds. It also moves from year to year with application volume, so a small shift says more about the applicant market than about the school. Two schools with similar published rates can treat your specific profile very differently. Use acceptance rates to size the overall difficulty of a list, not to estimate personal odds.

How do you build a safety, target, reach list?

Definitions first. A reach school's published GMAT center, its average or median, sits clearly above your score. A target school's center sits near your score, comfortably inside the middle 80%. A safety school's center sits clearly below your score. Selectivity at the most prominent programs makes them reaches for nearly everyone, whatever the number.

  1. Put every school on one yardstick. Convert your score and each published figure to a percentile before comparing, especially when one school reports Focus figures and another reports Classic ones.
  2. Sort with the range, not just the center. A score inside the middle 80% marks a target; a score below the bottom of the published spread marks a reach on numbers alone.
  3. Balance the list. A workable list mixes a few reaches, a core of targets, and at least one safety you would genuinely attend.
  4. Check the calendar. A list is only real if you can finish essays and recommendations for every school on it; our month by month MBA application timeline maps the backward schedule.
