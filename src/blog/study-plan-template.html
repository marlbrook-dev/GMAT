Every study plan that works, for any exam, is built from the same four phases: diagnose honestly, drill your weaknesses, review your misses on a spaced schedule, and finish with mock exams plus a taper. As a share of study time, a reliable default is 10 percent diagnosis, 40 percent targeted drills, 25 percent spaced review, and 25 percent mocks and taper. The phases never change. Only three knobs do: the weeks you have, the hours you can protect each week, and the format of your exam.

What are the four phases of every good study plan?

Strip away the branding and most successful plans, whether for the GMAT, the GRE, the LSAT, or a licensing exam, follow one skeleton. You measure where you are, you spend most of your hours on the skills that measurement exposed, you keep old misses alive through short daily reviews, and you rehearse the real event before you walk into it. Each phase feeds the next: the diagnosis picks the drills, the drills generate the misses, the misses fill the review queue, and the mocks check whether all of it holds under time pressure.

PhaseShare of study timeWhat you actually do
1. Diagnose honestlyAbout 10%Take one full-length practice test under real timing before you study. Score it by section and skill. Rank your weaknesses, set a target, and book a test date.
2. Targeted drillsAbout 40%Work your two or three weakest skills in timed sets of 10 to 15 questions, one skill at a time, then mixed. Log every miss: the skill, the trap, the fix.
3. Spaced reviewAbout 25%Spend 10 to 15 minutes daily reworking logged misses at expanding intervals: same day, two days, one week, two weeks. Retire questions only after a clean timed solve.
4. Mocks and taperAbout 25%Take full-length mocks in the final quarter, reviewing each as long as you took it. In the last week, cut volume, run short mixed sets, and stop two days out.

The shares are a starting point, not a law. A large score gap pulls time toward drills. A pacing problem pulls time toward mocks. What should not move is the order and the presence of all four phases, because each one exists to correct a specific way plans fail.

How do you diagnose honestly?

Take a full-length practice test under real conditions before you study anything. That order matters. The common instinct is to review first and test later so the baseline looks respectable, but the baseline is not a verdict, it is a map. An inflated one sends your drill hours to the wrong places.

Honest diagnosis means real timing, no pausing, no second guesses, and a scoring pass that goes deeper than the total. Break the result down by section and then by skill, and write three things down: your target, the gap, and a ranked list of your weakest areas. Then book the actual exam. A fixed date converts the rest of the template from intention into schedule.

What makes a drill targeted?

Targeted drills attack the weaknesses your diagnostic exposed, in small timed sets, with a written record of every miss. This phase earns the biggest share of your hours because it is where scores actually move, and it is also where untargeted plans quietly waste the most time.

The waste has a familiar shape: grinding the material you are already good at. Strengths feel productive because accuracy is high, but the points they add are few. Discipline here means working one weak skill at a time until accuracy improves untimed, then adding the clock, then mixing that skill into varied sets so you learn to recognize it cold. Throughout, keep an error log. One line per miss: which skill, which trap, what the fix is. That log is not paperwork. It is the input for the next phase.

Why review misses on a spaced schedule?

Your misses are the highest-value material you own, and a single correction does not keep them fixed. Massed practice creates short-term fluency that fades within days. Returning to a missed question after a gap forces effortful retrieval, and that effort is what makes the correction durable. The practical cadence is modest: 10 to 15 minutes a day reworking old misses before any new material, with each clean solve earning a longer wait until the next look and each repeat miss shrinking the wait back down.

The mechanism, the research behind it, and a full interval schedule are covered in our guide to spaced repetition. For the template, one rule is enough: no miss leaves the queue until you have solved it cold, under time, after a real gap.

How do mocks and a taper close the plan?

Mock exams answer the question drills cannot: does your skill survive the full event? Take them under complete test conditions, including the timing, the breaks, and the section order you plan to use on the day. Then honor the half of the phase most people skip: review. Budget as much time to analyze a mock as you spent taking it, and feed every miss into the same spaced queue.

The taper is the last piece. In the final week, cut volume rather than adding it. Run short mixed sets at test pace to stay sharp, confirm logistics, skim your error log summaries, and stop studying two days before the exam. Fatigue on test day costs more than any final session could add.

How do you adapt the template to your exam and calendar?

Three knobs adjust the template to your situation.

  • Weeks available. The phase shares stay constant while the calendar stretches or compresses. A 6 week window and a 16 week window both spend about 40 percent of their hours on drills; the longer window simply allows more spacing cycles and more mocks. For a worked example of this skeleton on one exam, see our realistic 8 week GMAT plan.
  • Hours per week. Your budget is weeks multiplied by weekly hours. When work limits you to a few hours a week, stretch the weeks rather than compressing the phases, and protect the daily review cadence above all, since it is the cheapest phase and the first one busy people drop.
  • Exam format. Learn the structure from the exam maker before you build the calendar, because format decides what a mock is and how long a drill set should run. The GMAT Focus Edition, for example, is three 45 minute sections scored 60 to 90 each, with a total of 205 to 805 (mba.com, GMAC, 2026), so its natural drill unit is a timed set within one section. ETS publishes the equivalent structure for the GRE at ets.org. Whatever your exam, the official page beats any summary of it.

The bottom line

Diagnose honestly, drill what the diagnosis exposed, review every miss on a spaced schedule, and rehearse the real event before you sit it. Split your hours roughly 10, 40, 25, and 25 across those phases, then adapt with the three knobs: weeks, weekly hours, and format. The template is deliberately plain because the failure points of study plans are plain: flattering baselines, comfortable drills, abandoned review, and skipped rehearsal. Close those four doors and almost any reasonable calendar will carry you to test day prepared.
