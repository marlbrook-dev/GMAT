An LSAT author's attitude question asks how the author regards something in the passage, and each answer choice names a feeling and what it is about. The right choice gets both halves: how strongly the author holds the view, and what the view is about. LSAC lists an author's attitude, as revealed in the tone of a passage or the language used, among the things its Reading Comprehension questions may ask about (LSAC, 2026).

What the Question Asks

LSAC's sample asks "Which one of the following best captures the author's attitude toward Lichtenstein's work?" Each of its five choices pairs a feeling with an aspect of the work, from "enthusiasm for its more rebellious aspects" to the answer, "appreciation for its ability to incorporate both realism and naiveté" (LSAC sample questions, 2026). LSAC rates it a middle difficulty question, by the number of test takers who answered it correctly when it appeared on the LSAT.

A Four Step Method

  1. Find the words that judge. LSAC suggests underlining descriptive words that help identify the author's attitude toward a particular idea or person (LSAC, 2026). Look at adjectives and adverbs, at verbs such as hid or failed, and at anything the author says should be done.
  2. Read the contrasts. What an author says the subject is not can show the attitude as clearly as praise. LSAC's explanation of its sample finds the author's appreciation partly in the claims that the work "transcended mere parody" and did not display a "jaded cynicism".
  3. Weigh praise against reservation. Decide which the author leaves standing. In LSAC's sample, the evaluations that settle the answer appear in the last paragraph and form part of the author's conclusion.
  4. Match both halves. The choice's feeling should be as strong as the author's language and no stronger, and it should be about what the author actually judges.

A Worked Example

In rehanging its gallery by the year each picture was painted, rather than by the region its painter came from, the Wrenmoor Museum has done its visitors a real service. A landscape now hangs beside a portrait painted in the same season, and a visitor can see at a glance which fashions crossed the mountains and which never did. The old arrangement hid this behind labels that sorted painters by birthplace.

The change has a cost the museum has not admitted. Painters who worked in one town for forty years are now scattered across six rooms, and a visitor who comes to see one of them must walk the whole building to do it. A plan by the entrance, marking where each painter's work now hangs, would answer most of this objection, and the museum should add one.

The author's attitude toward the rehanging is best described as

(A) approval tempered by a reservation about one of its effects

(B) unqualified enthusiasm for every part of the change

(C) disapproval of a change that scatters each painter's work across the building

(D) indifference to how a gallery arranges its pictures

(E) gratitude for the museum's frankness about what the change costs

The words that judge are "a real service", "hid", "a cost the museum has not admitted" and "should". The first paragraph praises the change, partly by contrast with the old arrangement, which hid what the new one shows. The second names a cost and proposes a remedy for it, a plan by the entrance, which is a way to keep the change rather than a call to undo it. So the author approves, with a reservation: (A). (B) is too strong, since the second paragraph is a real objection, and (D) is too weak, since the author plainly cares how the pictures hang. (C) takes the reservation for the whole attitude and drops the praise, which the author never withdraws. (E) is contradicted: the author says the museum has not admitted the cost. The answer is (A).

Sorting the Wrong Answers

KindWhat is wrong with itIn the example
Wrong strengthStronger or weaker than the author's language(B), (D)
Part for the wholeOne side of a mixed view, or an aspect the author treats as secondary(C)
ContradictedRests on something the passage denies(E)

LSAC's explanation of its sample rejects two choices of each of the last two kinds. Enthusiasm for the work's rebellious aspects and pleasure in its rejection of abstract expressionism both fix on its rebellion, which the author says was not all that characterized the work and which, LSAC says, the author does not regard as its most important aspect. Respect for its parody and admiration for its critique of contemporary culture rest on claims the passage denies: the author says the work transcended mere parody, and, in LSAC's words, very clearly says that Lichtenstein embraced contemporary culture.

Common Traps

  • Choosing a strong word because the view is clear. A clear view is not the same as a strong one: an author can be plainly in favor and still hold a reservation.
  • Reading only one paragraph's tone. A reservation in the second paragraph does not cancel praise in the first, and praise does not erase a stated cost.
  • Matching the feeling and ignoring its object. A choice can name the right feeling about the wrong thing, such as an aspect the author mentions but treats as secondary.

How Should You Practice?

When you finish a passage, ask two questions before you look at the choices: how strongly does the author hold the view, and what is it about?
