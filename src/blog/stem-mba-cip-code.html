There is one question an international MBA applicant can ask that changes the value of the degree more than essays, recommendations, or the score on the test. Almost nobody asks it, and it takes one sentence.

The question is: which CIP code is this program registered under?

What a CIP Code Is and Why It Decides Your Working Life

Every academic program in the United States carries a code from the Department of Education's Classification of Instructional Programs. It is administrative plumbing, invisible on the brochure and invisible in the ranking tables. It is also the single input that determines whether a graduate on an F-1 visa gets 12 months of work authorization after the degree or 36.

The Department of Homeland Security publishes a list of the fields it considers science, technology, engineering or mathematics for the purposes of the 24 month extension of optional practical training. If your program's code is on that list, you are eligible for the extension. If it is not, you are not, and no amount of institutional prestige changes it.

The Fact That Surprises People

CIP code 52.0201, "Business Administration and Management, General," is the code a conventional MBA carries. It is not on the DHS STEM Designated Degree Program List.

These are the only codes in the 52 series, "Business, Management, Marketing and Related Support Services," that appear on the list:

Codes and titles above are transcribed from the DHS STEM Designated Degree Program List, last updated July 22, 2024, published under 8 CFR 214.2(f)(10)(ii)(C)(2). The current version is at ice.gov, and DHS updates it periodically, so check it rather than trusting this table forever.

What the Designation Is Worth in Months

Post completion OPT gives up to 12 months of employment authorization. A degree on the DHS STEM list, with an employer enrolled in E-Verify and a completed Form I-983 training plan, adds a 24 month extension for a combined 36 months. Those figures come from USCIS.

Now put that against the H-1B cap. The regular annual cap is 65,000 new statuses, with an additional 20,000 for beneficiaries holding a master's degree or higher from a US institution, and registration is electronic and annual. A graduate with 12 months of work authorization gets one registration before the clock runs out. A graduate with 36 months gets three.

That is the whole argument. Three lottery entries instead of one, decided by a four digit code chosen before you enrolled.

How to Ask, Word for Word

Send this to every program on your shortlist:

"Which CIP code is your full-time MBA registered under in SEVIS, and does that code currently appear on the DHS STEM Designated Degree Program List? If some concentrations or tracks carry a different CIP code than the general MBA, which ones, and what are their codes?"

You will get one of three answers. The program is 52.0201 and is not STEM. The whole MBA is coded to a STEM code and every graduate qualifies. Or, most commonly, the general MBA is not STEM but particular tracks are, which means the concentration you pick in your first term decides your work authorization on graduation day.

What This Does Not Mean

A non STEM MBA is not a bad MBA. If you intend to return home after the degree, the code is irrelevant to you. If you are targeting a cap exempt employer, a university, an affiliated nonprofit, or a nonprofit or government research organization, the lottery is not your constraint either. The code matters precisely when your plan is to work in the United States for a private employer and you need the runway to get through a lottery that you may not win on the first attempt.

It is also not a substitute for a good application. Our month by month application timeline and the score bands at top programs still describe the work of getting in. This is about what the degree is worth once you are.

Where to Put the Answer

Record it next to each school while you are researching, not in your head. The application checklist has a column for exactly this once you turn on the international applicant toggle, and the international applicant guide covers the rest of the rules the code sits inside.

The worst outcome here is not getting a bad answer. It is finding out in your final term, from a classmate, that the track you did not choose was the one that carried the code.
