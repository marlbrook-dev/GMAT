The average GMAT score at MBA programs depends on which GMAT you mean. On the GMAT Focus Edition, the current exam, 26 programs in our school library publish an average or median for a class that entered in 2025 or 2026, from 541 at Colorado Leeds to 690 at Columbia Business School; on the Classic exam, 21 publish one, from 650 at Boston College Carroll to 740 at Yale SOM (school class profiles and Poets&Quants, 2025 and 2026). The two scales differ, so they stay in separate tables.

Why Do MBA Programs Report Two GMAT Scores?

Because their classes hold both. GMAT Focus Edition testing began on November 7, 2023 (GMAC, 2023), and a score from either edition stays valid for five years (GMAC, 2023), so the students who enrolled in 2025 and 2026 applied with a mix of the two. 20 of the programs above report both, 19 of them for the same class; MIT Sloan's GMAT Focus median is its Class of 2028's, and its Classic median, reported by Poets&Quants, the Class of 2027's.

Neither figure describes the whole class. Chicago Booth says which students each covers: 39 percent of its Class of 2027 submitted a GMAT Focus score, 14 percent a Classic score and 42 percent a GRE, and its ranges are the middle 80 percent of each group (Chicago Booth class profile, 2025). A program's GMAT Focus average is the average of its Focus takers, not of everyone who enrolled.

How Do You Compare the Two Editions?

By percentile, never by the raw number. GMAC's official concordance tables put a Classic 700 and a Focus 655 at the same point, the 90.5th percentile; a Classic 710 sits at about a Focus 665, and a Classic 740 at about a Focus 695 (GMAC concordance tables). A 675 on the GMAT Focus is not a weaker score than a 700 on the Classic because the number is lower. Our GMAT score chart walks through the scale and the concordance in full.

Some schools print a GMAT figure without naming its edition. Because a class can hold both and the scales differ, that figure could be on either scale or a blend of the two, so it is left out of these tables, and out of our school pages, rather than guessed at.

Averages, Medians and Ranges

Most figures are averages. Harvard Business School, Berkeley Haas, MIT Sloan and Yale SOM report medians for both editions, Texas McCombs a median for the GMAT Focus, and UCLA Anderson calls its figures a mean, the same statistic as an average. A median is the score of the student in the middle of the group, so a few very high or very low scores move an average but not a median.

The ranges schools print reach well below every headline figure. Columbia's GMAT Focus scores for the Class of 2027 run from 615 to 805, Stanford GSB's from 615 to 785 and Dartmouth Tuck's from 595 to 775, and where a school prints its middle 80 percent, about one student in ten sits below the bottom of it: 555 at Georgetown McDonough, 595 at Carnegie Mellon Tepper and BU Questrom, and 605 at UNC Kenan-Flagler (school class profiles and Poets&Quants, 2025 and 2026). An average is the middle of a class, not a cutoff.

How Should You Use These Numbers?

  • Find your target school in the table for the edition you took, and read its range or middle 80 percent, not only its average.
  • Compare an average with an average and a median with a median, and check which class each figure describes.
  • To weigh a score from the other edition, convert by percentile with GMAC's tables, never by the number.
