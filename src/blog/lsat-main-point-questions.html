An LSAT main point question asks which choice most accurately states what the passage as a whole says, and a primary purpose question asks what the author is doing in writing it. In both, the right answer covers the whole passage. LSAC's explanation of its own sample puts the trap plainly: for a choice to be the main point, it must do more than simply express a claim with which the author would agree (LSAC, 2026).

What the Questions Ask

LSAC lists the main idea or primary purpose first among the things its Reading Comprehension questions may ask about (LSAC, 2026). Its sample set has one of each. One asks "Which one of the following most accurately states the main point of the passage?", and the other completes "The primary purpose of the passage is most likely to". LSAC's explanations describe both tasks in terms of the passage as a whole.

A Three Step Method

  1. Say what each paragraph does. A few words each: states a view, gives evidence against it, offers a new explanation.
  2. Join them into one sentence. That sentence is your main point; the verb in it, such as challenges or explains, is the primary purpose.
  3. Match, then test for scope. The right choice covers every paragraph. A choice that covers one paragraph, one example or one concession is too narrow, however true it is.

A Worked Example

For decades the decline of the fishing fleet at Gullhaven was put down to the young men who left for work in the cities. The explanation seemed natural, since the fleet shrank in the same years the town's population aged. But the harbor's own records tell a different story. Boats were laid up not when crews left but when the inner harbor silted, so that only the smallest boats could enter at low tide. Families who kept their boats moved them to the deeper berths at Saltby Point, and their sons went with them.

None of this means the pull of city wages played no part, and some crews did leave before the harbor silted. But an account that begins with the harbor explains both the laid-up boats and the timing of the departures, while the older account explains only the departures.

Which one of the following most accurately states the main point of the passage?

(A) The Gullhaven fleet declined mainly because the inner harbor silted, and young men's leaving followed from that decline more than it caused it.

(B) Young men left Gullhaven for work in the cities during the years the fleet shrank.

(C) Some crews left Gullhaven before the inner harbor silted.

(D) Harbors with breakwaters tend to silt.

(E) Harbor records are more reliable than the memories of townspeople.

The first paragraph states the old explanation and the evidence that undercuts it; the second concedes a small point and weighs the two accounts. Joined, they say the harbor, not the young men, explains the decline. (A) says that and covers both paragraphs, so it is the answer. (B) is something both accounts accept, but it is the old account's premise, not the passage's point. (C) is the concession in the second paragraph. (D) is never stated, since the passage mentions no breakwater, and (E) compares two kinds of evidence the passage never compares. The answer is (A).

Sorting the Wrong Answers

KindWhat is wrong with itIn the example
True but too narrowOne detail, example or concession rather than the whole passage(B), (C)
Not in the passageA claim or comparison the passage never makes(D), (E)

LSAC's explanations of its samples reject choices of both kinds. For the main point question, it rejects choices that describe one example of a problem rather than the main point, that focus on only one of the issues the passage discusses, and that turn a hope mentioned in connection with one case into the passage's point. For the primary purpose question, it rejects choices because nowhere in the passage are two opposing theories discussed, because the passage does not cover what the choice describes, and because what the choice names is neither mentioned nor alluded to.

Common Traps

  • Picking the claim you remember best. The most vivid sentence can be a detail.
  • Picking a concession. A point the author grants is part of the passage, not its point.
  • Picking a purpose the passage never pursues, such as comparing two theories when it sets out only one view and its correction.

How Should You Practice?

After every passage you read, write its main point in one sentence before you look at the questions, then check it against the choices.
