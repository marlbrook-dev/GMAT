Only one of the five tests takes the choice away from you. LSAC reports every LSAT result from the current testing year and the five before it to each law school you apply to, cancellations and absences included (LSAC, 2026). The GRE, the SAT and the ACT let you choose which test dates a school gets, though ETS and College Board both warn that some programs ask for every score (ETS, 2026; College Board, 2026). The GMAT lets you decide whether to send each score, but since August 12, 2026 a report can also carry your best section scores from other attempts, in a GMAT Superscore you cannot leave off (GMAC, 2026). Here is what each school sees, and where your choice ends.

GMAT: You Choose Each Report, and the Superscore Comes With It

GMAT scores go out one report at a time. You review your Official Score in your mba.com account before deciding whether to send it, and schools and programs have no access to your official scores unless you choose to send them (mba.com, 2026). Up to five reports are free if you choose the programs within 48 hours of your Official Score becoming available (mba.com, 2026).

What changed on August 12, 2026 is what a report contains. GMAC now builds a GMAT Superscore from your highest section scores across all valid attempts of the current edition, at a test center or online. It sits on the same 205 to 805 scale as the Total Score and has no percentile of its own; GMAT 10th edition scores and expired scores are left out, and if two attempts share your highest score in a section, the more recent one is used (GMAC, 2026). If you have a Superscore, it is included automatically whenever you send a single attempt score, and you cannot opt out. The report a school receives then shows the Superscore, the Quantitative Reasoning, Verbal Reasoning and Data Insights scores used to build it, the exam date and delivery method of each, and the individual scores sent to that program in the past five years (GMAC, 2026).

So a school you send your second attempt to can see your best Verbal Reasoning score from your first, and when you took it. If you have taken the GMAT only once, or scored your best in all three sections on the same attempt, you have no Superscore, and the report makes that clear (GMAC, 2026). Scores canceled because of a testing issue or a policy violation also remain part of your Official Score Report (mba.com, 2026).

GRE: ScoreSelect Lets You Choose

On test day, after viewing your scores at the test center, you can send each of your four free score reports as Most Recent, the scores from that day, or All, your scores from every GRE General Test in the last five years, or send nothing yet. After test day, reports cost US$40 per recipient and add a third option, Any, which sends one or as many test administrations from the last five years as you like (ETS, 2026).

ETS adds a caution of its own: some programs require applicants who have taken a GRE test more than once to report the scores from all their GRE test administrations, and it recommends checking the score reporting requirements of each program you apply to (ETS, 2026).

LSAT: Every Result Goes to Every Law School

The LSAT is the one test where you do not choose. All of your LSAT results are reported to the law schools you apply to if they were earned in the current testing year or the prior five testing years, and LSAT results include scores, cancellations and absences. Testing years run from July through June, and results from before July 2021 are not reported. The score report carries the results of up to 12 reportable tests, a percentile rank for each score and your score band (LSAC, 2026).

Your only choice comes before a score joins your file. You can cancel within six calendar days of your test date, or buy LSAT Score Preview, $46 before the first day of testing or $87 once testing has concluded, and decide within six calendar days of the score's release whether to keep it; if you do nothing, the score is added to your LSAC file and released to schools (LSAC, 2026). A canceled score still appears on your report as a cancellation, as our guide to canceling a score explains.

SAT: Score Choice, by Test Date

With Score Choice you choose which scores you send to colleges, by test date (College Board, 2026). You cannot mix sections: a Math score from one test day and a Reading and Writing score from another cannot go together, because scores from an entire SAT are sent. Some institutions superscore, though, considering your highest section scores across test dates (College Board, 2026).

There are two limits. Score Choice is not available for the free score sends you may select when you register for a weekend SAT, or when you take the SAT as part of in-school testing. And some colleges and scholarship programs require you to send all your scores; as you choose what to send, College Board shows the policy of each school you have selected, so you can send what it requires (College Board, 2026).

ACT: A Single Test, Your Superscore, or Both

ACT sends your scores automatically to the high school and colleges you selected when you registered. After that, you decide what else goes out, to colleges and scholarship agencies at any time: the scores from an individual test event, your Superscore, or both. ACT calculates the Superscore automatically once you have tested more than once since September 2016, averaging your highest section scores across all test dates, and many colleges accept it. Colleges can receive scores from test events since September 2011 (ACT, 2026).

Each report is priced by test date: $20 per test date, per report, with a $32 archive fee for scores 3 or more years old (ACT, 2026). Our ACT Composite and superscore calculator shows how the Superscore is built from your sections.

Check Each Program Before You Send

The makers of the GRE and the SAT both say some programs want every score, and the program is the one to ask (ETS, 2026; College Board, 2026). On the GMAT, the question has changed shape: GMAC's own advice is to aim for your best total and section scores on every attempt, because a Superscore always travels with a single attempt score that schools see as well (GMAC, 2026).
