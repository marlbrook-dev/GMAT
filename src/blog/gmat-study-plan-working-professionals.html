One focused hour a day is enough to prepare for the GMAT Focus Edition, provided the hours are structured. The plan below pairs five short weekday sessions with one longer weekend block, about six to seven hours a week in total: an hour a day on average, with the Saturday block deliberately longer. At that pace, most working professionals can complete a full preparation in two to three months without borrowing time from their job or their sleep. This guide covers the daily routine itself: what each session contains, when to hold it, and what to drop when a work week turns heavy.

A note on scope before we start. This article is the routine, not the calendar. For the week-by-week arc, the total hours to budget, and when to book the exam, see our guide on how long to study for the GMAT. The two are designed to be used together.

Why do short daily sessions work for this exam?

The GMAT Focus Edition is a 2 hour 15 minute test: three 45 minute sections, Quantitative Reasoning, Verbal Reasoning, and Data Insights, each scored 60 to 90 and combining into a total score from 205 to 805 (mba.com, 2026). Every question type on it rewards fluency: recognizing a setup quickly and executing a known method under time pressure. Fluency is built by frequent, spaced retrieval, not by occasional long sittings. Five 45 minute sessions spread across a week beat one five hour Sunday, even though the totals match, because each session forces you to pull back what the previous one taught.

There is a second, plainer reason. An hour is defensible on a work calendar. A four hour block is not. Study plans usually fail at the scheduling layer before they fail at the content layer, and a plan you can hold on your worst week is worth more than an ambitious one you abandon in week three.

What does the weekly template look like?

Here is the default week. Move the days around to fit your calendar, but keep the shape: four working sessions, one light day, one deep block, one genuine rest day.

DayMinutesFocus
Monday45Quant: one skill, a timed set of 8 to 10 questions, then review
Tuesday45Verbal: one skill, timed set, then review
Wednesday45Data Insights: one skill, timed set, then review
Thursday45Weakest area of the week, plus an error log pass
Friday30Light retrieval only: flashcards and error log, no new content
Saturday120 to 150Deep block: a full timed section or two mixed sets, then full review
Sunday0 to 20Rest, or an optional short error log pass

Each weekday session keeps the same internal structure: about 25 minutes of timed questions on a single skill, 15 minutes of review, and 5 minutes updating an error log. The single-skill constraint matters. Mixed practice has its place in the weekend block, but a weekday session is too short to split. Pick one skill, such as rates problems or strengthen questions, and work only that.

Review is where the hour earns its keep, and review should be retrieval rather than rereading. Close the explanation, restate the solution path from memory, then check yourself. Our guide to active recall for the GMAT covers the method in detail. It is also the logic our trainer runs on: missed questions return on a spaced schedule and timing is tracked per question, so the error log largely maintains itself.

What is the weekend deep block for?

The Saturday block, roughly two to two and a half hours, does the work the weekday hours cannot. Sections on the GMAT Focus Edition run 45 minutes each, so pacing and stamina have to be trained in sittings at least that long. Use the block for one of two things: a full timed section followed by a complete review, or two mixed sets that combine the week's skills. Later in your preparation this is where full-length practice tests live, and a full test plus review will consume the whole block and part of Sunday.

Protect Sunday. A real rest day is not lost time; it is what makes Monday's session possible. If you must do something, cap it at 20 minutes with the error log.

Should you study in the morning or the evening?

The honest answer: whichever slot you actually control. The tradeoffs are still worth weighing.

  • Morning. Fewer cancellations, because meetings rarely claim the hour before work. You study fresh, ahead of the day's decision fatigue. The cost is an earlier bedtime, and that part is not optional; a morning slot funded by shorter sleep is a net loss.
  • Evening. A good fit when your evenings are predictable and your mornings are not. The risks are overruns at work and depleted focus. If you study at night, start the session as soon as you get home, before dinner. Sessions scheduled for "later tonight" have a way of not happening.

A split pattern also works: timed practice in the morning, 15 minutes of review in the evening. Whatever you choose, fix the slot and defend it like a meeting. A consistent time and place removes the startup friction that eats short sessions alive.

Can commutes and lunch breaks count?

They can carry real weight, under one rule: they are bonus review, never the core hour. A train or bus commute suits retrieval work: error log flashcards, solution paths restated from memory, one untimed question worked in your head. It does not suit timed sets, which need a desk, quiet, and scratch paper. If you drive, let the drive be rest; problem solving behind the wheel serves neither task well.

Lunch is more capable. Twenty quiet minutes at a desk fits a short timed set of five or six questions. If the office is loud, a booked meeting room or a nearby library is not. Two or three lunch sets a week, layered on top of the template, add up to an extra section's worth of practice without touching your evenings.

What do you cut when a work week explodes?

Some weeks the plan will take a hit. Cut in this order and the damage stays contained:

  1. Cut new content first. Skip the new skill and run the session as pure review of old misses. Ten minutes of retrieval preserves far more progress than it appears to.
  2. Shrink sessions, do not skip them. A 15 minute session on a brutal day keeps the habit alive. Zero days are the expensive ones, because each zero day makes the next one easier.
  3. Move the deep block last. If Saturday is gone, a shortened Sunday block beats nothing. If the whole weekend is gone, accept it and resume on Monday. No makeup marathons; they trade one bad week for two.

The one thing you never cut is sleep. Memory consolidates overnight, so an hour of study taken from sleep is worse than no study at all: it weakens both the next session and the next workday. In the final two weeks this becomes tactical. Shift your sleep so you are fully alert at your appointment hour, and rehearse the 2 hour 15 minute format at that same hour during your last two weekend blocks.

What makes this plan hold up?

None of this is dramatic, and that is the point. A study plan for a working professional is judged on a single criterion: does it still exist in week six? Six to seven modest hours a week, held steadily, will carry you through the material and into test shape. The plan's job is to make the next session easy to start. Yours is to start it.
