The GRE General Test runs about 2 hours and contains five scored parts: one Analytical Writing essay, two Verbal Reasoning sections, and two Quantitative Reasoning sections, for 54 questions plus the essay. Verbal and Quant are each scored 130 to 170 and are often read together as a 260 to 340 total, while the essay is scored 0 to 6 and reported separately (ETS, 2026). This guide covers what each section tests, how scoring works, and how to prepare.

What sections does the GRE have?

The GRE has three scored measures spread across five parts. Analytical Writing comes first and consists of a single timed essay. Verbal Reasoning and Quantitative Reasoning each appear as two separate scored sections. ETS shortened the exam in recent years, and the current version fits everything into about 2 hours (ETS, 2026).

Verbal ReasoningTwo sectionsReading comprehension plus text completion and sentence equivalence, with a heavy vocabulary load130 to 170
Quantitative ReasoningTwo sectionsThe standard high school math toolkit, including geometry, with an on-screen calculator available on every question130 to 170

All figures describe the current shortened GRE General Test (ETS, 2026). ETS publishes the exact per section timing and question counts on the official GRE page, and those details can shift, so confirm them there before you book. For planning purposes the totals are what matter: 54 questions, one essay, about 2 hours in the chair.

Two Verbal question types deserve a definition, because neither has a GMAT counterpart. Text completion presents a short passage with blanks and asks you to fill them so the meaning holds together. Sentence equivalence asks for the two answer choices that complete a sentence in the same way. Both reward a large reading vocabulary. On the math side, GRE quant covers more ground but is generally gentler than GMAT Focus quant, and it keeps geometry, which the GMAT Focus Edition dropped entirely (mba.com, GMAC, 2026).

How is the GRE scored and reported?

You receive three numbers, not one. Verbal Reasoning and Quantitative Reasoning are each scored 130 to 170. Applicants often add them into an informal 260 to 340 total, but ETS reports them separately, and admissions committees read them separately. The Analytical Writing essay is scored 0 to 6 on its own line and never joins the other two (ETS, 2026).

Percentiles carry the meaning. The same scaled number can sit at very different percentile ranks in Verbal and in Quant, so read each score against its own percentile table rather than treating the two scales as interchangeable. For a sense of where selective programs land, the Harvard Business School Class of 2027 reported a median GRE of 164 Quant and 164 Verbal (HBS Class of 2027 profile, 2025).

Score reporting is flexible in ways the scales are not. ETS lets you choose which scores schools see, and GRE scores remain valid for a multi year window; the current policies and fees live on the official GRE page at ets.org. One caution for MBA applicants: never compare a GRE number to a GMAT number directly. The scales share nothing, so compare percentiles, and use our GMAT vs GRE comparison when choosing between the exams.

Is the GRE adaptive?

Yes, at the section level. Section adaptive means your performance on the first Verbal section determines the difficulty of the second Verbal section, and the same holds for Quant. Within any section you can move freely: skip a question, return to it, and change answers until the section clock runs out. The GMAT Focus Edition takes the opposite approach, adapting question by question, with bookmarking and up to 3 answer changes allowed per section (mba.com, GMAC, 2026).

The practical consequence is a difference in pacing strategy. On the GRE, the first section of each measure sets up the second, so early questions deserve full care, and free movement makes a two pass approach viable: answer what comes quickly, then circle back to what does not. Test takers who like to skip and return usually find the GRE format the more comfortable of the two.

Who tends to prefer the GRE?

Acceptance is not the deciding factor, because business schools take both exams. In the Harvard Business School Class of 2027, 44% of students submitted a GRE score, the largest single group of test submitters (HBS Class of 2027 profile, 2025). The GRE also reaches beyond business school: most non-business graduate programs accept it, which the GMAT cannot claim. Beyond that, certain profiles lean GRE for concrete reasons:

  • Multiple applications. One GRE score can support an MBA application and a policy, data science, or other master's application in the same cycle.
  • Strong vocabulary. Heavy readers tend to find text completion and sentence equivalence friendlier than GMAT critical reasoning.
  • Calculator comfort. The GRE allows the on-screen calculator on every quant question. The GMAT Focus Edition allows one only in Data Insights (mba.com, GMAC, 2026).
  • Writers. The GRE keeps a scored essay, which suits applicants who want a writing sample on record. The GMAT Focus Edition has none.

If several of these describe you, the GRE is probably your exam. If none do, take one timed practice test for each and let the percentile results make the call.

How should you prepare for the GRE format?

The format is particular; the learning science is not. Preparation that works for any adaptive exam works here: start with a full-length diagnostic, train in timed sets, log every miss, and review those misses on a spaced schedule so skills still hold on test day. Spaced review matters even more on the GRE than on most exams, because vocabulary crammed in a week evaporates, while words revisited at growing intervals stay put.

Split content work into three lanes matching the three measures. For Quant, rebuild the standard toolkit, geometry included, and practice with the on-screen calculator so it speeds you up instead of tempting you into brute force. For Verbal, pair steady reading practice with a vocabulary review deck that resurfaces old words on a schedule. For Analytical Writing, draft full essays under the clock rather than reading about essays. Then rehearse the section adaptive rhythm with full-length practice tests, since pacing across two sections per measure is a skill of its own.

If you are arriving from GMAT prep, more transfers than you might expect: arithmetic, algebra, word problems, and reading comprehension all carry over. What you must add is vocabulary, geometry, and the essay, so budget several weeks for format-specific work. The bottom line

The GRE General Test is a section adaptive exam of about 2 hours: one scored essay, two Verbal sections, and two Quantitative sections, 54 questions in all, with free movement inside each section (ETS, 2026). Verbal and Quant each report on a 130 to 170 scale, the essay reports separately on a 0 to 6 scale, and percentiles do the real talking. Business schools accept it alongside the GMAT, and most other graduate programs accept it too. Pick the exam whose percentiles favor you, prepare with timed practice and spaced review, and confirm the operational details on ets.org before you book a seat.
