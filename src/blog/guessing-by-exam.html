Yes, on all five. LSAC and ACT say in so many words that a wrong answer costs nothing, ETS's scoring of the GRE's Verbal and Quantitative measures counts the questions you answer correctly, College Board says that for most students a guess is better than a blank, and GMAC counts the number of questions you answer as one of the three factors in every GMAT section score. Here is each rule in its test maker's own words, and what each test lets you do with a question you want to come back to.

GMAT: The Number You Answer Is Part of the Score

GMAC bases the Quantitative Reasoning, Verbal Reasoning and Data Insights scores on the same three factors: the number of questions answered, whether the answers are correct or incorrect, and the difficulty and other parameters of the questions answered (GMAC, 2026). A question you never answer is missing from the first of those factors, which is why finishing each section matters. An answer also counts only once it is submitted: you click Next and then Yes to confirm, and if both steps are not completed before time runs out, the response is not saved or scored (GMAC, 2026).

You can still change your mind about a guess. As you move through a section you can bookmark questions to look at later, and once you have answered every question in it you reach its Question Review and Edit screen, where you can review as many questions as you like and change up to three answers. If no time remains in the section, you skip that screen and move on (GMAC, 2026). Our guide to GMAT time management covers pacing a section so that screen is still open to you.

GRE: Only Right Answers Are Counted

ETS says the scoring for the Verbal Reasoning and Quantitative Reasoning measures takes into consideration the total number of questions answered correctly across the two sections, as well as the difficulty level of the sections (ETS, 2026). A wrong answer and a blank leave that count where it was, and a right guess adds to it.

The GRE also makes guessing easy to revisit. Within a section you can skip questions, go back and change answers, and use its Mark and Review features to tag questions to return to if you have time remaining in the section (ETS, 2026). Our GRE format guide covers the rest of the test.

LSAT: No Deduction for Incorrect Answers

LSAC is the plainest of the five. Your LSAT score is based on the number of questions you answered correctly, your raw score; all test questions are weighted exactly the same; and there is no deduction for incorrect answers. The raw score is then converted to the LSAT scale of 120 to 180 (LSAC, 2026). Which questions you get right does not matter, only how many, so an easy question and a hard one are worth the same point. Our LSAT format and scoring guide has the rest.

SAT: A Guess Usually Beats a Blank

College Board's guide to fall 2026 scores says your scores depend on several factors, including whether you answer questions right or wrong, the difficulty level of the questions, and the probability that you might be guessing. Its advice is still to guess: for most students, it is better to guess than leave a question blank, especially if you can eliminate one or two wrong answers first (College Board, 2026).

Within a module you can move back and forth between questions, mark any of them for review, and review your answers until time expires, but once you have moved on from a module you cannot go back to it (College Board, 2026). So a question you mean to return to has to be settled before you leave its module. Our digital SAT format guide explains how the modules work.

ACT: No Penalty for Guessing

Your ACT scores are based on the number of questions you answer correctly, and there is no penalty for incorrect answers (ACT, 2026). ACT's own guide to the 2026 to 2027 test tells you to answer every question, and if you do not know how to work toward an answer, to guess, flag the question and move on to questions you know how to do, coming back to the flagged ones if there is time (ACT, 2026). Our ACT format guide covers each section.

How to Guess Well

The test makers' own advice comes down to three habits. Eliminate what you can before you guess, as College Board and ACT both suggest. Mark the questions you guess on so you can return if time allows: the GRE and SAT let you mark questions for review, ACT tells you to flag them, and the GMAT lets you bookmark them and change up to three answers per section. And make sure every question has an answer before time runs out, because on none of the five does a blank earn anything.
