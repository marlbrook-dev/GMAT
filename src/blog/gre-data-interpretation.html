A GRE Data Interpretation set is a group of Quantitative Reasoning questions that all refer to the same table, graph or other display of data, and asks you to interpret or analyze it. The questions can be either kind of multiple choice or Numeric Entry, and ETS's tips for them are about reading the display: scanning it, checking its units and scales, and answering only from what it shows (ETS, 2026).

ETS's Tips for a Data Set

  1. Scan, do not study. Look briefly at what the display is about, then focus on the parts each question needs.
  2. Read the labels. Pay attention to the axes and scales of graphs, the units of measurement or orders of magnitude, such as billions, in titles, labels and legends, and any notes that clarify the data.
  3. Read graphs by their scales. Read, estimate or compare quantities by sight or by measurement against the scales, using the relative sizes of bars or sectors, and watch for broken scales and for bars that do not start at 0.
  4. Answer only from the data. Use the data, everyday facts such as the number of days in a year, and your knowledge of mathematics, and not specialized information you happen to know about the subject unless it can be derived from the data.

A Worked Example

The table shows the bicycles a shop sold in three years, in thousands.

YearBicycles sold (thousands)
202312
202415
202518

1. By what percent did sales rise from 2023 to 2024? The rise is 3 thousand on a base of 12 thousand, and 3 is 25 percent of 12. The answer is 25.

2. How many bicycles did the shop sell in the three years together? 12 plus 15 plus 18 is 45, but the table counts in thousands, so the answer is 45,000. Entering 45 is the trap, and it is the one ETS's tip about orders of magnitude in titles and labels is there to catch.

3. In which years did sales rise by more than 20 percent on the year before? Indicate all such years. (A) 2024 (B) 2025. 2024 rose 25 percent on 2023. 2025 rose 3 thousand on 15 thousand, which is exactly 20 percent, and exactly 20 is not more than 20. The answer is (A) alone, and a year that sits on the line is the reason to compute rather than judge by eye.

Where Data Questions Go Wrong

SlipWhat happensETS's tip
Missing the unitsThe right figure, a thousand times too smallRead titles, labels and legends for units
Trusting a bar's heightA broken scale or a bar not starting at 0 misleadsRead the scale, not the picture
Bringing in outside factsAn answer the data does not supportUse only the data, everyday facts and mathematics

How Should You Practice?

Before the first question of a set, say aloud what the display measures and in what units.
