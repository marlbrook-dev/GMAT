An MBA class profile is a school's statistical snapshot of its newest class: test scores, GPA, class size, work experience, and demographics. Reading one well comes down to three checks: whether each score is an average or a median, which GMAT edition a number belongs to, and what population each percentage describes. Miss those and the same page that should calibrate your plan will mislead it. Here is how to read each figure the way an admissions office publishes it.

Check 1: Average or Median?

Schools choose their own statistic, and the choice changes what a number means. An average is pulled by outliers; a median is the middle student. Stanford GSB reports an average GMAT, Classic 738 and Focus 689 for the Class of 2027 (Stanford GSB class profile, 2025), while MIT Sloan reports medians, Classic 720 and Focus 675 (MIT Sloan class profile, 2025). Those four numbers describe two schools with broadly similar classes; the statistic, not just the student body, drives part of the gap. When you build a school list, note the statistic next to every score, the way our rankings library does with a source and year on each figure.

Check 2: Which GMAT Edition Is That Number?

Since the 2024 format reset, classes contain both GMAT Focus Edition scores (205 to 805) and Classic scores (200 to 800), and profiles list them separately. The editions cannot be compared digit for digit: on GMAC's official concordance a Classic 700 corresponds to a Focus 655, both at the 90.5th percentile (mba.com, 2026). A profile showing "GMAT Focus 675" and "GMAT 730" is not showing a 55-point spread in ability; it is showing the same class measured on two scales. Our guide to the GMAT scoring scale covers the concordance in detail.

Check 3: What Population Does Each Percentage Describe?

Percentages on a class profile describe the enrolled class, not the applicant pool, and definitions move between schools. One school's international share counts foreign nationals; another counts anyone with a non-US passport, including dual citizens and permanent residents. Employment figures have the same trap: "employed at three months" can mean offers received or offers accepted, and schools state which in their employment reports. When a school does not publish a figure at all, treat the blank as information about the school's disclosure habits, never as a number to fill in from a forum.

How to Use a Profile Without Being Used by It

A class profile is context, not a cutoff. Scoring inside the middle 80 percent range means the school regularly admits people with your numbers; it promises nothing. Scoring below it means the rest of the application has to work harder, which is exactly the calculus behind a sensible reach, target, and safety split. Set your own target score from the schools on your list, as we outline in what counts as a good GMAT score, and recheck profiles each fall: schools publish new ones every cycle, and the numbers move.
