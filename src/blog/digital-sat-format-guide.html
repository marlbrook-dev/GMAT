The digital SAT runs 98 questions in 2 hours 14 minutes and scores 400 to 1600, with Reading and Writing and Math each scored 200 to 800 (College Board, 2025). Its defining feature is not the screen but the structure: each section arrives as two equal modules, and how you perform on the first decides whether the second is the harder or the easier form. That one design choice changes how the first half of every section should be played.

How Does Section-Adaptive Delivery Actually Work?

A section-adaptive test adjusts once per section rather than after every answer. You take the first module, the test scores it, and it routes you into one of two second modules: a harder form or an easier one. Both count toward your section score, but the harder form is the one that reaches the top of the scale, which is why the first module carries weight out of proportion to its question count.

Two practical consequences follow. First, inside a module nothing is locked: the questions are fixed in advance, so you can skip, flag, and change answers freely until the module clock ends. Use that. Answer everything on a first pass, flag what you want to revisit, and spend the recovered time on the flags. Second, because there is no penalty for a wrong answer, a blank and a miss cost exactly the same, while a considered guess can score. Never leave a question empty.

College Board does not publish the threshold that decides the routing, and it does not publish the equating tables that convert raw performance into a scaled score. Any specific cutoff or raw-to-scaled chart you find online is an estimate, however confidently it is presented.

What Is Actually Tested in Each Section?

The College Board sorts every question into one of eight content domains, four per section, and publishes how many questions each domain contributes to a full section. Which Order Do the Questions Come In?

Neither section shuffles. In Reading and Writing, a module opens with Craft and Structure questions and then moves through Information and Ideas, Standard English Conventions, and Expression of Ideas, with questions testing similar skills grouped together and arranged easiest to hardest inside each group. In Math, the whole module runs easiest to hardest.

That ordering is a pacing instrument, not trivia. If you are 18 questions into a Reading and Writing module and behind the clock, you know the grammar block is coming and that those questions are typically the fastest points on the section. If you are grinding at Math question 6, you are spending your best time on questions the test considers easy, and the expensive ones are all still ahead.

What Is Different About Math Entry and Tools?

Roughly a quarter of Math questions are student-produced responses, or grid-ins, where you type a value rather than choose one. They accept any equivalent form, so 1/2, 0.5, and .5 all score. Negative answers are allowed; percent signs and units are not. The Desmos graphing calculator is built into the Bluebook testing app and is allowed on the entire Math section, along with annotation, answer elimination, and question flagging (College Board, 2025). A built-in calculator does not make estimation obsolete: typing costs seconds, and on a section that gives you about 95 seconds per question, the habit of rounding first and calculating second is still worth more than the tool.

How Should That Change Your Practice?

Three adjustments follow directly from the structure. Practice in modules, not in loose sets, so the first-module pressure that drives routing becomes familiar rather than novel. Track your accuracy by content domain rather than as one number, because the ranges above tell you a point of improvement in Algebra is worth roughly twice a point in Geometry and Trigonometry. And practice in the digital format you will actually test in; paper materials misstate the pacing, the tools, and the freedom to move inside a module.
