An LSAT flaw question asks how an argument's reasoning goes wrong, and the right answer names the gap between what the evidence shows and what the conclusion claims. Identifying flaws in arguments is one of ten skills LSAC lists for its Logical Reasoning questions (LSAC, 2026). Two tests sort the choices: does the argument actually do this, and is it the reason the conclusion fails?

What a Flaw Question Asks

LSAC's sample flaw question asks for the choice that completes "The reasoning in the political scientist's argument is flawed because it", and its explanation describes the task as identifying how the reasoning in the argument is flawed (LSAC sample questions, 2026). The premises are not in question. A flaw is a fault in the step from the premises to the conclusion: the evidence could all be true and the conclusion still not follow.

A Four Step Method

  1. Find the conclusion. Say in a few words what the argument sets out to show.
  2. Find the evidence. Say what it offers in support, and nothing more.
  3. Name the gap. Ask what the conclusion claims that the evidence does not show, and put the answer in your own words before you read the choices.
  4. Test each choice twice. First, does the argument actually do what the choice says? Second, is that the reason the conclusion does not follow? A right answer passes both tests.

A Worked Example

Towns in the county that have opened a public library in the past ten years now have lower crime rates than towns that have not. So opening a public library reduces crime.

The reasoning in the argument is flawed because it

(A) overlooks the possibility that some other difference between the towns, such as how prosperous they are, accounts for both the new libraries and the lower crime

(B) fails to say how many books the new libraries hold

(C) assumes that crime fell in every town that opened a library

(D) attacks the people who oppose new libraries rather than their reasons

(E) treats something that is needed for lower crime as if it were enough to bring lower crime about

The conclusion is that opening a library reduces crime. The evidence is a difference between two groups of towns. The gap is that a difference between groups does not show what caused it: towns that can afford a new library may differ from the others in ways that also affect crime. (A) names that gap, and the argument does overlook it, so (A) passes both tests. (B) is true of the argument, since it says nothing about books, but the number of books has nothing to do with whether the conclusion follows, so (B) fails the second test. (C), (D) and (E) describe things the argument never does: it compares groups of towns rather than claiming anything about each one, it mentions no opponents, and it never says that anything is needed for lower crime. They fail the first test. (D) and (E) are the harder ones to set aside, because each names a real flaw; this argument just does not commit either. The answer is (A).

The Wrong Answers to Expect

KindWhy it is wrongIn the example
Something the argument never doesFails the first test(C)
A real flaw, but not this argument'sFails the first test, and sounds right because the flaw is familiar(D), (E)
True of the argument, but not a flawFails the second test(B)

LSAC's explanation of its sample rejects a choice of each kind. It rejects one because the political scientist does not express a personal point of view, something the argument never does. It rejects another because the argument does not indicate that any conditions are necessary or sufficient for what it discusses, so it could not be said to confuse those two sorts of conditions: a real flaw, but not this argument's. And it rejects a third even though the argument does fail to consider what that choice describes, because that does not constitute a flaw in its reasoning.

Common Traps

  • Picking a choice because it names a familiar flaw. A choice that names a real flaw the argument does not commit is still wrong.
  • Picking something the argument leaves out. An argument leaves out many things, and most of them do not matter to whether the conclusion follows.
  • Arguing with a premise. LSAC's advice is to answer on the basis of the information given, even if you do not agree with it (LSAC, 2026), and a flaw question asks what is wrong with the reasoning built on that information.

How Should You Practice?

For each official flaw question you work, write the gap in a few words before you read the choices, then run the two tests on every choice and note which one each wrong answer fails.
