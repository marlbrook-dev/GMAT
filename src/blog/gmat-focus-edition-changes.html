The GMAT Focus Edition is the current version of the GMAT. It replaced the Classic exam in 2024, and the new GMAT format is shorter and simpler: three equally weighted sections instead of four, 64 questions, and 2 hours 15 minutes of testing (mba.com, 2026). The essay, Sentence Correction, and geometry are gone. A new section, Data Insights, counts in the total score, which now runs 205 to 805. If your prep materials predate 2024, the first job is an audit.

What changed in the 2024 format reset?

The reset was not a tweak. GMAC rebuilt the exam around three sections, Quantitative Reasoning, Verbal Reasoning, and Data Insights, each scored 60 to 90 and weighted equally in the total (mba.com, 2026). The table below puts the two editions side by side.

FeatureGMAT Classic (retired)GMAT Focus Edition
SectionsFour: Analytical Writing, Integrated Reasoning, Quantitative, VerbalThree: Quantitative Reasoning, Verbal Reasoning, Data Insights
QuestionsAbout 80, including one essay task64: 21 Quant, 23 Verbal, 20 Data Insights
LengthJust over 3 hours2 hours 15 minutes, plus one optional 10 minute break
EssayYes, the Analytical Writing AssessmentNone
Total score scale200 to 800205 to 805, in 10 point steps, always ending in 5
Review featureAnswers locked once confirmedBookmark and change up to 3 answers per section

Two mechanics deserve attention. Test takers pick the order of the three sections, and each section runs exactly 45 minutes. Within a section, you can bookmark questions and change up to 3 answers before time runs out (mba.com, 2026). The Classic exam locked every answer the moment you confirmed it, so older pacing advice never accounts for a review pass.

What was removed from the exam?

Three familiar pieces of the old test are gone, and each removal should change how you spend study hours.

The essay

The Analytical Writing Assessment no longer exists. There is no writing task of any kind on the Focus Edition. If your study plan reserves time for essay templates or practice prompts, reclaim it.

Sentence Correction

Verbal Reasoning no longer includes Sentence Correction. Grammar drills, idiom lists, and error logs built around parallelism and modifiers do not pay off on this exam. Verbal now rewards careful reading and argument analysis, so Reading Comprehension and Critical Reasoning practice is where that time should go.

Geometry

Quantitative Reasoning dropped geometry. Triangles, circles, and coordinate geometry proofs are off the syllabus, and the section centers on arithmetic and algebra. Old problem sets are still usable, but skip the geometry chapters rather than grinding through content the exam will not test.

What does Data Insights absorb?

Data Insights is the structural heart of the reset. It absorbs the content of the old Integrated Reasoning section and the Data Sufficiency questions that used to live inside Quant (mba.com, 2026). Two things make this more than a renaming. First, Integrated Reasoning was scored separately on the Classic exam and never touched the 200 to 800 total; Data Insights counts fully, as one third of the 205 to 805 total. Second, Data Sufficiency now sits alongside chart, table, and multi-source material rather than among standard math problems, which changes how the questions feel under time pressure.

The section has 20 questions in 45 minutes, the same time allotment as Quant and Verbal (mba.com, 2026). Treat it as a discipline of its own. Our Data Insights guide walks through each question type and how to pace them.

Why does old 200 to 800 advice mislead?

Because the scales do not map one to one: on GMAC's official concordance tables a Classic 700 corresponds to a Focus 655 at the same percentile, so an old book telling you that 700 is the magic number is quietly pointing at a far rarer score than its author meant. The safe habit is to translate everything into percentiles, and our GMAT score chart and percentiles guide lays the two scales side by side so you can do that in seconds.

How should you adjust your prep?

Four changes cover most of the gap between a Classic era plan and a current one.

1. Audit your materials

Check the publication date on every book and question bank. Anything built before 2024 needs a trim: remove Sentence Correction, geometry, and essay content entirely. What survives, core arithmetic, algebra, Reading Comprehension, and Critical Reasoning, is still good practice.

2. Give Data Insights a full third of your schedule

The section carries the same weight as Quant and Verbal, yet many self study plans treat it as an afterthought because Integrated Reasoning once was one. Practice Data Sufficiency and the absorbed Integrated Reasoning formats under timed conditions from week one, not in a final cram.

3. Set your target in percentiles

Start from the concordance, not from remembered Classic numbers. If your goal was framed as "break 700" on the old scale, the equivalent ambition on the Focus scale is a 655, and anything a school publishes should be read with its edition label and its average or median status in mind.

4. Rehearse the new mechanics

Take timed sections at 45 minutes, which works out to roughly two minutes per question in each section. Decide your section order in advance and keep it consistent. Practice using the bookmark tool and the 3 answer change allowance deliberately: it rewards flagging a doubtful answer and moving on, a habit the Classic exam punished.

The reset rewards test takers who read the current rules instead of inherited ones. The exam is shorter, the syllabus is narrower, and the scale is new. Prepare for the test that exists now, and let the 2024 materials cutoff be your filter.
