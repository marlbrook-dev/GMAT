On September 15, 2026, the rule that governed how long international students may stay in the United States changed for the first time in decades. If you are applying to a US MBA program, this is now part of your planning rather than background.

The change comes from a Department of Homeland Security final rule, "Establishing a Fixed Time Period of Admission and an Extension of Stay Procedure for Nonimmigrant Academic Students, Exchange Visitors, and Representatives of Foreign Information Media," published at 91 FR 44976 on July 17, 2026 and effective September 15, 2026. The official text is at govinfo.gov. Everything below is drawn from it.

Duration of Status Is Gone

For decades an F-1 student was admitted for "duration of status," an open ended admission that lasted as long as the student remained enrolled and in compliance. That is over. F and J nonimmigrants are now admitted for a fixed time period set at "up to the program length, not to exceed a 4-year period."

For a standard two year MBA this changes little in practice. Where it bites is at the edges: a dual degree, a deferral, a leave of absence, a program that runs long. Under the old rule those were handled by staying enrolled. Under the new rule they require an approved extension of stay.

The Provisions That Matter to an MBA Applicant

ProvisionWhat the Rule Says
Length of admissionUp to the program length, capped at four years.
Departure preparationCut from 60 days to 30 days after completing a course of study or an authorized period of post completion practical training.
Ending earlyIf you end study or training early, you and eligible dependents must depart or act to maintain lawful status within 30 days of the end date.
TransferringF-1 students at the graduate level or above are prohibited from transferring at any point during a program of study, unless SEVP authorizes an exception for extenuating circumstances.
Changing objectivesGraduate level and above F-1 students are prohibited from changing educational objectives at any point during a program of study.
Moving levelsA student who completes a program at one level may only begin another at a higher level. Same or lower level is prohibited while an F-1 student.
Program delaysA delay in finishing by the I-20 end date, including delays from academic probation or suspension, is "generally an unacceptable reason for program extensions."
Pending extensionA timely filed extension of stay lets you continue a full course of study after your admission period expires, until USCIS adjudicates it.
Practical training reprieveFor the first six months after the rule takes effect, students applying for employment authorization for post completion OPT or STEM OPT get a short term reprieve from filing for an extension of stay.
BiometricsMay be collected in connection with an extension of stay application, as required by 8 CFR 103.16.

The Transfer Restriction Changes How You Should Shortlist

This is the provision with the biggest effect on how you apply. Under the old regime, starting at one graduate program and moving was uncommon but possible. Now, at the graduate level, it is prohibited outright during a program of study absent an SEVP authorized exception for extenuating circumstances.

Practically: choose the school you intend to finish at. A shortlist built on the assumption that you can course correct after a year is built on an assumption that no longer holds. That raises the value of doing the research properly the first time, which means reading class profiles rather than rankings alone and asking programs direct questions before you commit.

Thirty Days, Not Sixty

The halving of the departure preparation window is easy to skim past and expensive to get wrong. After you complete your program, or after an authorized period of post completion practical training ends, you have 30 days rather than 60 to prepare to depart. The same 30 days applies if you end study or training early.

If your plan involves a gap between the end of OPT and the start of something else, that gap is now half as forgiving as it was.

One Thing That Has Not Changed, and One Proposal That Might

OPT itself is untouched by this rule: 12 months of post completion OPT, plus 24 more if your degree sits on the DHS STEM list and your employer is enrolled in E-Verify. Which brings the CIP code question back into focus, because a shorter departure window makes a longer work authorization window worth more.

Separately, on September 11, 2026 DHS published a proposed rule to remove the discretionary grace period of up to 60 days that certain nonimmigrants receive when employment ends before their authorized stay expires (91 FR 57807). It is a notice of proposed rulemaking, not law: written comments are due on or before November 10, 2026, and nothing has changed yet. It would matter later, to an MBA graduate working on H-1B whose job ends, rather than to a student today.

What to Do About It

Three things, none of which require a lawyer to start:

  • Read the rule rather than a summary of it, including this one. It is long, but the summary of changes is readable and it is the actual text you will be held to.
  • Build your shortlist as though you cannot transfer, because at the graduate level you cannot. Our application timeline assumes you are choosing once and choosing carefully.
  • Talk to the designated school official at any program that admits you, before you accept. They administer this rule daily and their answer is worth more than any article.
