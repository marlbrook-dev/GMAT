A GRE Sentence Equivalence question gives one sentence with one blank and six answer choices, and asks for the two choices that each complete the sentence coherently and produce sentences that mean the same thing. The pair you want is not simply two synonyms: ETS warns that a synonym pair can fail to fit the sentence, and that the right pair need not mean exactly the same thing (ETS, 2026).

How the Questions Are Built

ETS says each question consists of a single sentence with one blank and six answer choices, and you select two; there is no credit for partially correct answers. Like Text Completion, Sentence Equivalence tests your ability to reach a conclusion about how a passage should be completed from partial information, but to a greater extent it focuses on the meaning of the completed whole.

A Four Step Method

  1. Read the sentence for its overall sense. Before the choices, know what the sentence is saying.
  2. Find the words that matter. ETS points to words that show the structure of the sentence, such as although or moreover, and words central to what it is about.
  3. Fill the blank yourself, then look for two matches. If you find one word close to yours but no second, ETS's advice is not to fixate on your own reading: look for other words among the choices that fill the blank coherently.
  4. Check both sentences. Each choice should produce a sentence that is logically, grammatically and stylistically coherent, and the two sentences should mean the same thing.

A Worked Example

The mayor's critics had expected her to defend the plan to the end, so they were surprised when she _____ it within a week of the first complaints.

(A) championed (B) abandoned (C) dropped (D) praised (E) extolled (F) drafted

The words that matter are "expected her to defend the plan to the end" and "surprised": what she did was the opposite of defending it. In your own words, she gave the plan up. Two choices say that: abandoned, (B), and dropped, (C). They are not exact synonyms, since abandoned is the stronger word, but each produces a sentence that means she gave the plan up, which is all that counts. Praised and extolled, (D) and (E), are a closer pair of synonyms than the answer, but praising the plan is what the critics expected, so neither fits the surprise. Championed, (A), is the defense they expected, and drafted, (F), makes no sense for a plan that already existed. The answer is (B) and (C), and a response with only one of them earns no credit.

Common Traps

  • Hunting for synonyms first. A matching pair of words is the first trap ETS names; check that the pair fits the sentence before you choose it.
  • Rejecting a right answer because its partner is not a perfect synonym. What must match is the meaning of the two completed sentences.
  • Choosing one answer and guessing the other. There is no credit for one correct choice out of two, so a question is only finished when both choices fit.

How Should You Practice?

Fill the blank in your own words before you look at the six choices, then look for two that say the same thing in the sentence.
