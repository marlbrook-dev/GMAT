Spaced repetition means reviewing material at growing intervals instead of packing every repetition into one sitting. Daily rounds win because each short session asks your memory to work at the moment it is about to fail, and effort at that moment is what makes a memory last. For the GMAT Focus Edition, where three sections each score 60 to 90 (mba.com, 2026) and stored patterns matter as much as live reasoning, a modest daily habit outperforms a weekend marathon of the same total hours.

Where does the spacing effect come from?

In the 1880s, Hermann Ebbinghaus memorized lists of invented syllables and tested himself after set delays. Two of his observations have held up through more than a century of follow-up research. First, forgetting is steep at the start and then levels off. Second, repetitions spread out over time produce more durable memory than the same number of repetitions packed together. That second observation is the spacing effect, and it is among the most consistently replicated findings in the psychology of learning, confirmed across ages, subjects, and materials, from vocabulary to arithmetic to motor skills.

The mechanism, described in plain terms: a memory that has begun to fade takes real effort to retrieve, and that effort strengthens it far more than an easy repetition does. Review too soon and retrieval is trivial, so little is gained. Review too late and the memory is gone, so you are relearning from scratch rather than reinforcing. Spacing is the craft of timing each review into the useful window between those extremes. Forgetting is not the enemy of this system. Forgetting is the tool that makes each review count.

Why does cramming feel better than it works?

Massed practice, the formal name for cramming, produces fast and visible progress. Do ten rate problems in a row and the tenth feels easy. Reread a chapter the same evening and every page feels familiar. The trouble is that this fluency reflects momentary accessibility, not lasting storage. Researchers in the tradition of Robert Bjork draw exactly this distinction, separating how retrievable a memory is right now from how deeply it has been stored, and they describe spacing as a desirable difficulty: a condition that feels worse during practice but leaves more behind.

This explains why cramming survives despite its record. It gives feedback that is honest about the session and misleading about the future. The student who crams judges learning by how the evening felt. The student who spaces judges it by what survives to next week. Only the second judgment predicts test day, which on the Focus Edition arrives as three 45-minute sections (mba.com, 2026) with no warm-up on familiar material.

How do expanding intervals work?

Most spaced systems use expanding intervals. Each successful recall earns a longer delay before the next review, because the memory has proven it can survive that long. A failed recall shrinks the delay back down. Over a few cycles, material you know drifts out to reviews weeks apart, while stubborn material keeps returning until it sticks. Your limited study time flows toward exactly what you do not yet know.

What should GMAT students space?

Almost everything on the exam has a memory component, but three categories repay spacing most.

Formulas and quant facts

The Focus Edition dropped geometry (mba.com, 2026), but Quantitative Reasoning still leans on a body of retrievable facts: number properties, divisibility rules, exponent laws, the standard setups for rate, work, and mixture problems, and the basic counting templates. These behave like vocabulary. They fade along the Ebbinghaus curve, and they respond well to short daily reviews. A five-minute round of formula recall before problem practice keeps the toolbox stocked so your session time goes to reasoning, not to reconstructing basics.

Verbal and Data Insights patterns

Verbal Reasoning rewards recognition of recurring argument structures: causal claims, sampling assumptions, plans measured against goals. Data Insights rewards knowing, on sight, whether a Data Sufficiency prompt needs a specific value or only a relationship. Patterns fade just as facts do, and spacing decides when you meet them again. What you do in that meeting is the other half of the method: testing yourself cold rather than rereading notes. That half is covered in our guide to active recall for GMAT prep, and the two techniques are strongest together.

Your own errors

An error log is a personal spaced repetition deck, and it is the highest-value one you own, because every entry is a documented gap between what you believe you know and what you produced under pressure. The cadence matters more than the format. Revisit a miss the same day, again within the week, and again before test day. Spacing also argues for a longer runway: a plan measured in weeks leaves room for several full review cycles, which is one reason our guidance on how long to study for the GMAT favors steady schedules over compressed ones.

Does daily really beat a weekly marathon?

For the same total hours, distribution wins. Six separate half-hour rounds put more successful, effortful retrievals into the useful window than one three-hour block, and they build the habit of performing on demand rather than after a long ramp-up. The exam itself favors the daily pattern: 2 hours and 15 minutes of sustained recall under a clock (mba.com, 2026), with no credit for how well you studied the night before. Cramming keeps one legitimate job, a light refresh in the final days, and even that works best as the last step of a spaced plan rather than a substitute for one.
