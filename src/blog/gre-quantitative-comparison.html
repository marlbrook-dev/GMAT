A GRE Quantitative Comparison question gives two quantities, Quantity A and Quantity B, and asks which of four statements describes them: Quantity A is greater, Quantity B is greater, the two are equal, or the relationship cannot be determined from the information given. The skill is deciding the relationship without more arithmetic than it needs, and ETS gives five tips for it (ETS, 2026).

How the Questions Are Built

ETS lists Quantitative Comparison first among the four types of question on the Quantitative Reasoning measure, beside two kinds of multiple choice and Numeric Entry. A comparison question always has the same four answer choices, so you can learn them once. ETS singles out the last one: never select it if it is clear the values of the two quantities can be determined by computation, and once you have decided which quantity is greater, take care not to reverse the first two choices.

ETS's Five Tips

  1. Know the answer choices. They never change, so the question is only ever which of the four relationships holds.
  2. Avoid unnecessary computations. Simplify, transform or estimate either quantity only as far as you need to compare them.
  3. Do not trust a figure's look. Figures are not necessarily drawn to scale. Redraw one, keeping what the information fixes and changing what it leaves open, and see what varies.
  4. Plug in numbers. For algebraic quantities, try easy values, and try all kinds: zero, positive and negative numbers, small and large numbers, fractions and decimals. If Quantity A is greater in one case and Quantity B in another, the relationship cannot be determined.
  5. Simplify the comparison. Treat the two quantities like the two sides of an equation and simplify step by step, as you would when solving one, until the relationship is plain.

Two Worked Examples

1. x is not 0. Quantity A: x2. Quantity B: x.

Plug in numbers of different kinds. If x is 2, Quantity A is 4 and Quantity B is 2, so A is greater. If x is 1/2, Quantity A is 1/4 and Quantity B is 1/2, so B is greater. One case each way settles it: the relationship cannot be determined. A student who tried only 2 and 3 would have answered that Quantity A is greater.

2. Quantity A: 3(n + 4) - 2. Quantity B: 3n + 9.

No value of n is needed. Simplify Quantity A to 3n + 10, then take 3n from both sides of the comparison: 10 against 9. Quantity A is greater whatever n is, so this is one of the questions where choosing that the relationship cannot be determined would be wrong, because computation settles it.

How Should You Practice?

On every comparison with a variable, test at least one number of a different kind from your first before you answer.
