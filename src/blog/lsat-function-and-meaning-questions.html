A function question asks why the author includes a part of the passage, and a meaning in context question asks what a word or phrase means as the author uses it. Both are answered from the sentences around the part, not from what it says on its own or what the word usually means. LSAC lists the organization or structure of a passage, and the meaning or purpose of words or phrases as used in context, among the things its Reading Comprehension questions may ask about (LSAC, 2026).

What the Questions Ask

LSAC's single-passage samples include one of each (LSAC sample questions, 2026). The function question asks why the author lists some of the themes and objects in Roy Lichtenstein's paintings, completing "primarily to", and LSAC's explanation says the task is to identify from the context what the author is trying to accomplish by the list. The meaning question asks what the phrase "standard pricing pattern" most nearly means, and its explanation says the phrase is used by the author in a particular way. LSAC rates the first easy and the second difficult, by the number of test takers who answered each correctly.

How to Answer a Function Question

  1. Find the part and read around it. Read its own sentence and the one before. LSAC's explanation of its sample finds the answer in both: the listed themes and objects "were reflections of the culture Lichtenstein inhabited", and the sentence before says his work displayed "an impulse toward realism".
  2. Say what the part does for the paragraph. An example of a claim, evidence for it, a concession, a contrast or a definition. Say it in a few words before you read the choices.
  3. Keep the purpose to this part. A purpose another part of the passage serves is wrong here, and so is one the part cannot serve by itself.

How to Answer a Meaning Question

  1. Cover the choices and define the phrase from the passage. In LSAC's sample the author goes on to explain the pattern, and that explanation is the definition.
  2. Expect the other senses of the word. The wrong choices in LSAC's sample are other things standard can mean: a benchmark, an agreed criterion, merely acceptable, an exemplar.
  3. Put your choice back into the sentence. It should say what the author said, with nothing added.

A Worked Example

Historians have long treated the 1690 survey of Thornwick as a census, and its figure of four hundred households appears in every account of the town. But the figure is a conservative estimate. The surveyors counted only houses that paid the hearth tax, and the parish register for the same year records baptisms in families living in barns, cellars and the rooms above shops, none of which paid it. A town counted this way will always look smaller than it was.

None of this makes the survey useless. Set beside the survey of neighboring Ashwell, made the same year by a clerk who counted every dwelling, Thornwick's figure is low, but its streets and trades are described with a care the Ashwell survey never matches.

1. The author mentions families living in barns, cellars and the rooms above shops primarily to

(A) show why the survey's count of households is likely to be too low

(B) describe the conditions in which Thornwick's poorest families lived

(C) contrast the Thornwick survey with the survey of Ashwell

(D) argue that the hearth tax fell unfairly on the poor

(E) suggest that the parish register overstates the size of the town

2. The word "conservative" as used in the first paragraph most nearly means

(A) attached to tradition

(B) cautious, and so likely to understate

(C) politically right of center

(D) intended to preserve what already exists

(E) restrained in style

For question 1, the families come between the claim that the figure is conservative and the conclusion that a town counted this way will always look smaller than it was. They are the households the survey missed, so they show why the count is too low: (A). (B) is what the detail says, not why it is there. (C) is the job of the second paragraph, and the families play no part in it. (D) is never argued, since the families paid no hearth tax at all, and (E) reverses the passage, which uses the register to show the survey counts too few. The answer is (A).

For question 2, the sentences that follow define the word: only houses that paid the tax were counted, so the town looks smaller than it was. A conservative estimate here is a cautious one that errs low: (B). The other four are senses the word can have elsewhere, tradition, politics, preservation and style, and nothing in the passage supports any of them. The answer is (B).

Sorting the Wrong Answers

KindWhat is wrong with itIn the example
Content, not purposeSays what the part says, not why it is there1 (B)
Served elsewhereA job other text does, even a nearby sentence1 (C)
Not in the passageA claim the passage never makes, or reverses1 (D), (E)
Another senseA meaning the word has, but not in this sentence2 (A), (C), (D), (E)

LSAC's explanation of its function sample rejects three choices for giving the list a job other text does. The comparison with abstract expressionism is located in paragraph two and does not involve the list, and the list by itself does not suggest the emotions at the heart of the work or explain the painter's attitude toward consumer culture: it is how he treated the themes and objects that does. It rejects the fourth because the author does not claim the work was parodic in intent. Its explanation of the meaning sample rejects a benchmark because nowhere does the author suggest pricing patterns are measured against one another, an agreed criterion because the point is conformance to a historical pattern, not to agreed-upon standards, merely acceptable because there is no reason to think the author views pricing patterns as acceptable or unacceptable, and an exemplar because no particular case is offered as a model.

Common Traps

  • Choosing the answer that restates the part. A function question asks what the part is for, and a choice that only describes it is not an answer.
  • Choosing a purpose from the wrong paragraph. A comparison, concession or conclusion elsewhere in the passage is not the job of the part the question points to.
  • Choosing the meaning you know best. The usual sense of a word is the trap when the author uses it in a particular way.

How Should You Practice?

When a question points to a part of the passage, read that sentence and the one before it again before you look at the choices, and say in a few words what the part does or what the word means there. The LSAT trainer asks what a part of the passage is doing there on every one of its long passages, and our guides to main point questions and to the author's attitude cover the whole-passage questions these parts serve.
