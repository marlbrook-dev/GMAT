The LSAT consists of four 35-minute multiple-choice sections: two scored Logical Reasoning sections, one scored Reading Comprehension section, and one unscored variable section that can appear anywhere, plus a separate unscored Argumentative Writing sample (LSAC, 2026). Scores run 120 to 180, the raw score is simply the number of correct answers, and there is no penalty for wrong answers. Registration is $253 for the testing year that began in August 2026. Every figure here comes from LSAC.

What Is on the LSAT?

Since the August 2024 LSAT, the scored test is two parts argument analysis and one part dense reading: there is no logic games (Analytical Reasoning) section, and no math (LSAC, 2026).

A one-sentence definition worth keeping: the variable section is an unscored, unidentified section LSAC uses to pretest future questions, which means you must treat all four multiple-choice sections as if they count.

How Is the LSAT Scored?

The scale runs 120 to 180. Your raw score is the count of correct answers across the three scored sections, converted to the scaled score; wrong answers cost nothing beyond the missed point (LSAC, 2026). That single rule drives the most important habit in LSAT pacing: never leave a question unanswered. A score stays reportable for up to five testing years after the testing year in which it was earned, and your score report shows a percentile rank beside it; our LSAT percentile calculator looks up any score in LSAC's current table.

Where you sit the test has changed. Starting with the August 2026 LSAT, LSAC is moving the multiple-choice test into test centers for almost all test takers, with remote testing kept for limited exceptions, such as certain disability-related accommodations and test takers far from a center (LSAC, 2026).

Fees and Retake Limits

Registration is $253 for the August 2026 to June 2027 testing year, including the writing sample, and it costs the same for first-time and repeat test takers (LSAC, 2026). Retakes are capped at five within the current reportable period and seven in a lifetime, and a test taker holding a perfect 180 from the current period may not sit again (LSAC, 2026). Plan the calendar backward from application deadlines: the caps make each sitting count.
