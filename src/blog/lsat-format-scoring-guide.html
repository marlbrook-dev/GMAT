The LSAT consists of four 35-minute multiple-choice sections: two scored Logical Reasoning sections, one scored Reading Comprehension section, and one unscored variable section that can appear anywhere, plus a separate unscored Argumentative Writing sample (LSAC, 2025). Scores run 120 to 180, the raw score is simply the number of correct answers out of roughly 75 to 80 scored questions, and there is no penalty for wrong answers. Registration is $253 for the testing year that began in August 2026. Every figure here comes from LSAC.

What Is on the LSAT?

Since the current format took effect, the scored test is two parts argument analysis and one part dense reading; there is no logic-games section anymore, and no math.

A one-sentence definition worth keeping: the variable section is an unscored, unidentified section LSAC uses to pretest future questions, which means you must treat all four multiple-choice sections as if they count.

How Is the LSAT Scored?

The scale runs 120 to 180. Your raw score is the count of correct answers across the three scored sections, about 75 to 80 questions, converted to the scaled score; wrong answers cost nothing beyond the missed point (LSAC, 2025). That single rule drives the most important habit in LSAT pacing: never leave a bubble empty. Scores stay reportable for about five years, and delivery is a choice between a live remote-proctored test and an in-person Prometric testing center (LSAC, 2025).

Fees and Retake Limits

Registration is $253 for the testing year beginning August 2026, including the writing sample, versus $248 the prior year (LSAC, 2026). Retakes are capped at five within the current reportable period and seven in a lifetime, and a test taker holding a perfect 180 from the current period may not sit again (LSAC, 2025). Plan the calendar backward from application deadlines: the caps make each sitting count.
