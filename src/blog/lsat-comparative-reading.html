A comparative reading set gives you two related shorter passages instead of one, and its questions concern how the two relate. The method is to sum up each passage, name the relationship, list where the authors agree and where they part, and check both passages for every answer choice. A Reading Comprehension section includes either one comparative reading pair or none, alongside three or four single passages (LSAC, 2026).

How Comparative Reading Works

LSAC says comparative reading questions concern the relationships between the two passages, such as generalization and instance, principle and application, or point and counterpoint. Its sample pair, adapted from articles published in the mid-1990s, sets a passage warning about global warming beside one arguing that an extreme view of it has developed, and comes with seven questions (LSAC sample questions, 2026). LSAC's explanation of one of them says a significant number of comparative reading questions require an ability to infer what the authors' views are and how they compare, some about points of agreement and others about points of disagreement.

What the Questions Ask

The seven sample questions ask:

  • which question is central to both passages;
  • what passage B mentions that passage A does not, which LSAC says tests the ability to recognize a significant difference in content;
  • what the authors would most likely disagree over;
  • which thing cited in passage A is an instance of something passage B refers to;
  • which criticism the author of passage B would most likely make of predictions cited in passage A;
  • which pair of documents stands in the most analogous relationship;
  • how the argument in passage A relates to the argument in passage B.

A Four Step Method

  1. Sum up each passage on its own. One sentence each, as you would for a single passage.
  2. Name the relationship. Do the passages share a topic and part over one question, does one state a principle and the other apply it, does one answer the other?
  3. List where they agree and where they part. Before the questions, note what both accept and what they dispute. LSAC's explanation of its disagreement question says it is a good idea to recall what you have already concluded about points of agreement and disagreement when you read the choices.
  4. Check both passages for every choice. A disagreement needs a view from each author, and something mentioned in one passage but not the other has to be absent from the other, not only present in the first.

A Worked Example

Passage A: Since the town of Kesford closed its main street to cars in 2019, the street has come back to life, and the closure deserves the credit. Shops on the street report more customers than in any year before it, and the cafés that put tables out where cars once parked now stay open into the evening. The town's own counts show the largest gains on Saturdays, the day the street had been most choked with traffic.

Passage B: No one disputes that Kesford's main street is busier than it was. What is in doubt is why. Foot traffic began to rise in the spring of 2019, months before the street was closed to cars, when a new railway station opened at the edge of town, and side streets the closure never touched grew busier just as fast. A closure that explains neither the timing nor the side streets cannot take the credit.

The authors of the two passages would be most likely to disagree over

(A) whether more people now walk along Kesford's main street than before 2019

(B) whether closing the main street to cars is what made it busier

(C) whether the new railway station brought more visitors into Kesford

(D) whether the cafés on the main street now stay open later than they did

(E) whether the main street should be reopened to cars

Both authors accept that the street is busier: passage A says it has come back to life, and passage B begins by saying no one disputes it. So (A) is a point of agreement. They part over the cause. Passage A says the closure deserves the credit, and passage B says a closure that explains neither the timing nor the side streets cannot take it. That is (B). (C) is taken up only by passage B and (D) only by passage A, and in each case the other author says nothing, which is not the same as disagreeing. (E) is taken up by neither passage. The answer is (B).

Sorting the Wrong Answers

KindWhat is wrong with itIn the example
Both agreeThe authors share this view, sometimes only by implication(A)
One is silentOne author takes a view and the other says nothing about it(C), (D)
Neither speaksNeither passage gives a sign of either author's view(E)

LSAC's explanation of its own disagreement question rejects choices of all three kinds. Both authors would agree that natural events can cause changes in global climate, passage B directly and passage A indirectly. Passage A says nothing about how warmer air affects ocean temperatures, so there is no evidence its author would disagree with passage B on that. And the passages give no specific indication of either author's view on how much global temperatures have risen. A fourth choice fails because it is not clear the authors would disagree at all: passage B concedes there is some evidence that melting has occurred.

Common Traps

  • Treating silence as disagreement. If one author says nothing on a point, the passages give no evidence that the authors disagree on it.
  • Taking what is central to one passage as central to both. In its question about what is central to both passages, LSAC rejects one choice because passage B makes no mention of fossil fuels and another because passage A does not mention variations in the sun's radiation and the earth's orbit.
  • Assuming the second passage refutes the first. LSAC rejects a choice saying passage A makes assertions that passage B demonstrates to be false, because passage B does not demonstrate that any of them are false.

How Should You Practice?

Before you read the questions on a pair, write one sentence for each passage and a short list of what the authors share and where they part.
