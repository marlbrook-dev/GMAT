An LSAT strengthen question asks which new fact, if true, would make an argument's conclusion more likely. The method is to find what could still make the conclusion false despite the evidence, and choose the answer that rules it out. Determining how additional evidence affects an argument is one of ten skills LSAC lists for its Logical Reasoning questions (LSAC, 2026).

What a Strengthen Question Asks

LSAC's sample asks "Which one of the following, if true, most strengthens the argument?" (LSAC sample questions, 2026). Two words in that stem matter. "If true" means you accept each choice as a fact, however surprising, and ask what it does to the argument. "Most" means you compare the choices: the right answer need not prove the conclusion, only make it more likely than any other choice does.

How LSAC's Sample Works

In LSAC's sample, an argument concludes that a scientific theory is wrong because a search by many of the most sensitive instruments ever developed has found no trace of something the theory predicts. LSAC's explanation points out that failing to find evidence does not necessarily show that the evidence is not there: the instruments might not be powerful enough to detect it. So the argument would be strengthened by evidence that the instruments could in fact have found the thing if it existed. That is the pattern to look for: find what could still make the conclusion false, and look for the choice that rules it out.

A Four Step Method

  1. Find the conclusion and the evidence. Say each in a few words.
  2. Ask what could go wrong. What could be true that leaves the evidence standing but makes the conclusion false? Another cause, a poor measure or an unrepresentative sample are the kinds of thing to look for.
  3. Look for the choice that rules it out. Or for one that adds support the evidence was missing.
  4. Check the direction of every choice. Each one strengthens, weakens or does nothing to the conclusion. Only the first kind can be right.

A Worked Example

Since the Halbury council moved its street sweeping from the mornings to the nights, complaints about litter have fallen by a third. So sweeping at night keeps the town's streets cleaner.

Which one of the following, if true, most strengthens the argument?

(A) The town's streets are swept by a private firm under contract to the council.

(B) Inspectors who count the litter on the same streets each month have recorded less of it since the change.

(C) Several nearby towns also sweep their streets at night.

(D) Some residents find the noise of the night sweepers disturbing.

(E) At the same time as the change, the council began charging a fee to lodge a complaint.

The conclusion is that the streets are cleaner, and the evidence is that fewer people complain. What could go wrong is that complaints fell for some reason other than cleaner streets. (B) rules that out by measuring the litter directly, so it is the answer. (E) does the opposite: a fee for complaining would explain fewer complaints whether or not the streets are any cleaner, so it weakens the argument. (A) and (C) have no bearing on whether night sweeping cleans better. (D) is about a different question, whether night sweeping is a good idea on balance, and says nothing about how clean the streets are. The answer is (B).

Run the same argument the other way and the method still works. Asked which choice most weakens it, you would look for the choice that makes the conclusion less likely, and that is (E). Our trainer drills both directions.

Sorting the Wrong Answers

KindWhat it doesIn the example
Wrong directionMakes the conclusion less likely(E)
No bearingStays on the topic but does nothing to the conclusion(A), (C)
A different questionBears on something near the conclusion, not the conclusion itself(D)

LSAC's explanation of its sample rejects choices of all three kinds. One supports the theory the argument attacks, so it would, if anything, weaken the argument. One has no direct bearing on the question of whether the predicted object was produced. And one concerns objects that arise some other way, which LSAC calls irrelevant to the claim the argument is about.

Common Traps

  • Picking a choice that weakens the argument. Under time pressure a choice that matters to the argument can feel right in either direction, so check which way it pushes.
  • Dismissing the right answer because it does not prove the conclusion. It only has to make the conclusion more likely than the other choices do.
  • Picking a choice about a nearby question, such as whether a plan is a good idea, when the conclusion is about whether it works.

How Should You Practice?

For each official strengthen question you work, write down what could go wrong with the argument before you read the choices, then sort every choice by direction. Official questions are listed in our guide to free official practice tests, and the LSAT trainer drills the effect of additional evidence between them.
