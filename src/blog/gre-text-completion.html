A GRE Text Completion question omits crucial words from a short passage and asks you to choose the words or short phrases that make it a coherent, meaningful whole. The method ETS describes is to read the whole passage first, find the words that signal its structure, fill the blanks in your own words, and only then look for matches among the choices (ETS, 2026).

How the Questions Are Built

ETS says each passage is composed of one to five sentences, with one to three blanks per sentence. If there are two or three blanks, each blank has three answer choices; if there is only one blank, there are five. Each blank has a single correct answer, the choices for different blanks work independently, and there is no credit for partially correct answers. ETS says about half of the Verbal Reasoning measure requires you to read passages and answer questions on them, and the other half requires you to read, interpret and complete existing sentences, groups of sentences or paragraphs, which is the work Text Completion asks for.

A Four Step Method

  1. Read the whole passage. Get an overall sense of it before you look at a single choice.
  2. Find the words that matter. ETS points to words that show the structure, such as although or moreover, and words central to what the passage is about.
  3. Fill the blanks yourself. Put in a word or phrase of your own, then see whether a similar one is among the choices. ETS advises against trying every combination of answers, which takes too long and invites error.
  4. Start where the passage is clearest, then check. ETS says you need not fill the first blank first. Once every blank is filled, make sure the passage is logically, grammatically and stylistically coherent; if none of the choices for another blank makes sense, go back and reconsider your first selection.

A Worked Example

Although the committee's report was praised for its (i) _____, running to four hundred pages of evidence, its recommendations were so (ii) _____ that no department could tell what it was being asked to do.

Blank (i): (A) brevity, (B) thoroughness, (C) wit

Blank (ii): (D) precise, (E) vague, (F) costly

The words that matter are "although", which sets the praise against a failing, "four hundred pages of evidence", which describes the report, and "no department could tell what it was being asked to do", which describes the recommendations. Blank (ii) is the clearer, so start there: recommendations nobody can act on are unclear, so the word is vague, (E). Precise says the opposite, and costly would not leave anyone unsure what to do. Blank (i) is a virtue that four hundred pages of evidence displays and that "although" sets against vague recommendations: thoroughness, (B). Brevity is ruled out by the length, and nothing in the sentence supports wit. Read back, the sentence holds together. The answer is (B) and (E), and a response with only one of them earns no credit.

Common Traps

  • Judging each blank alone. Brevity is a fine word to praise a report with; it fails only against the four hundred pages, so a choice has to fit the whole passage, not just its own sentence.
  • Missing the structure word. Although, but and yet set two ideas against each other, and moreover and indeed carry one forward, so the word changes what a blank must mean.
  • Stopping before the check. A passage can read well one blank at a time and still not hold together; ETS's last step is to read the completed passage for logic, grammar and style.

How Should You Practice?

For each question, write your own words for the blanks before you look at the choices, then compare.
