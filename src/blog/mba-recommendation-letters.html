Ask the people who supervised your work most closely, not the people with the biggest titles. Admissions committees read MBA recommendation letters for specific, first-hand evidence: what you did, how you compare to peers, how you respond to feedback. A direct supervisor who watched you lead a project writes a stronger letter than a CEO who knows your name. Choose one or two close observers, brief them well, give them at least eight weeks, and never draft the letter yourself.

Who should write your MBA recommendation letters?

An MBA recommendation letter is a structured evaluation, written by someone who has supervised your work, answering a school's questions about your performance, leadership, and response to feedback. Most full-time programs ask for one or two letters and supply their own prompts through an online form. Confirm each school's current requirements on its official admissions page, for example the Harvard Business School admissions site, before you approach anyone.

The default first choice is your current direct supervisor. No one else has watched you work at close range, week after week, under real stakes. The default second choice is a former supervisor, a client, or a project lead from another team: anyone who has directly overseen deliverables you produced. If telling your current manager would put your job at risk, schools understand. Pick the closest observer you can and explain the substitution briefly in the application's optional section.

What committees are not looking for is altitude. A letter from a founder, a senior partner, or a famous graduate carries no weight if the writer cannot describe your work unprompted. Apply a simple test before you ask: can this person name two specific things you did in the past year and explain how you did them? If the answer is no, the title does not matter. Specificity beats seniority every time.

What separates a strong recommender from a weak one?

Strength is a property of the relationship, not of the writer's resume. Run each candidate through the checklist below before you ask.

TraitStrong recommenderWeak recommender
Working relationshipSupervised you directly on real deliverablesKnows you mainly by reputation, title, or org chart
EvidenceCan cite two or three of your projects, with outcomes, without promptingOffers general praise: hardworking, bright, dedicated
Comparative contextHas managed enough people to rank you honestly against peersHas no reference class to compare you against
InvestmentAsks about your goals and blocks time to writeAgrees reluctantly or wants to hand the writing back to you
ReliabilityAnswers email and meets deadlinesChronically slow to respond, likely to go quiet in deadline week

Two rows deserve emphasis. Comparative context matters because many school prompts ask the writer to rate you against others they have managed, and only someone who has led real teams can answer that credibly. Reliability matters because a brilliant letter that arrives after the deadline is worth nothing. A slightly less senior writer who will actually deliver is the better pick.

How do you prepare recommenders ethically?

Preparation means giving your writers context, never words. Three inputs, delivered as a short packet, cover it:

  • Your goals. One paragraph on why an MBA, why now, and which schools. Writers align their letters with a story they can actually see.
  • Specific projects. A current resume plus three or four projects you worked on together, with dates, your role, and the results. You are restoring their memory, not scripting their opinions. Which examples they use, and what they conclude, stays their call.
  • Logistics. The list of schools, each deadline, how the forms will arrive, and roughly how much time each one takes.

What you must not do is write the letter yourself, even if the recommender offers, and busy recommenders offer often. The line is worth stating plainly. Applications ask the recommender to certify that the letter is their own work, so a ghost-written letter is fabricated evidence submitted under someone else's name. Schools treat that as an integrity violation, and offers can be withdrawn over it.

Ghost-writing also backfires on the merits. Committees read your essays and your letters side by side, and a candidate who writes both produces documents that share vocabulary, framing, and favorite examples. Readers who see thousands of files spot the overlap quickly. Worse, a self-written letter loses the only thing a recommendation adds: an outside observer's independent detail. If a recommender asks you to draft it, offer the packet and a conversation instead. If they still will not write it themselves, they have told you something useful. Ask someone else.

How should you manage the recommendation timeline?

Letters fail on schedule more often than on content. Work backward from your first deadline:

  • Eight to ten weeks out: ask in person or on a call, not through a forwarded form. Confirm the yes is an enthusiastic one.
  • Same week: send the packet: goals, projects, schools, deadlines.
  • Four weeks out: check in, answer questions, and resend anything that got lost.
  • Two weeks out: confirm the school forms arrived and drafts are moving.
  • One week out: ask writers to submit early, so a travel week or a crashed portal does not cost you the round.

Recommendations run in parallel with everything else, so protect the calendar as a whole. Settle your test early: our GMAT versus GRE for MBA guide covers that choice, and our guide to how long to study for the GMAT shows why prep alone occupies a 6 to 12 week window. Asking for letters during your final practice-test weeks, with essays still unwritten, is how applications slip to the next round.

After you submit, close the loop. Tell your writers the outcome and thank them properly. They spent hours on your future, and you may need them again.

The bottom line

Choose the one or two people who have watched your work most closely and will finish on time. Brief them with your goals, the specific projects you shared, and a clear schedule, then let them write in their own voice. Ask eight to ten weeks out and build in a buffer week. A specific letter from a direct supervisor beats a vague letter from an impressive title, and an honest process protects the offer you are working this hard to earn.
