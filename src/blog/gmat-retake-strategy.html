Retake the GMAT when three things are true at once: your total sits 20 or more points below the median or average at your target schools on the GMAT Focus Edition scale of 205 to 805, you can name the specific reason the first attempt fell short, and at least four to six weeks remain before your deadline. When any one of the three fails, the hours usually do more good elsewhere in the application. Here is the full framework, ending in a scenario table.

How big is the gap to your target score?

Your target is not an abstract number. It comes from the class profiles your schools publish. Harvard Business School reports a median GMAT Focus Edition score of 685 (HBS Class of 2027 profile, 2025), and MIT Sloan reports a median GMAT Focus of 675 with a middle 80 percent of 645 to 735 (MIT Sloan Class of 2027 profile, 2025). Stanford GSB reports an average GMAT Focus of 689 (Stanford GSB Class of 2027 profile, 2025). Note which is which: Stanford and Wharton publish averages, while MIT and HBS publish medians. Our guide to what counts as a good GMAT score in 2026 walks through the full landscape.

With a target set, the arithmetic is simple. At or above the school's published number, a retake buys little, and the essays and recommendations need those hours more. Twenty or more Focus Edition points below it, you are a genuine retake candidate. In between sits a judgment call: a 10 or 15 point shortfall matters more for a candidate with a thin profile than for one with strong work experience and grades.

One edition note. If your score comes from the Classic exam, compare editions by percentile, never by raw number. GMAC's concordance tables map Classic scores to Focus Edition scores that sit at the same percentile. A Classic 700 concords to a Focus Edition 655, both at the 90.5th percentile (GMAC concordance tables). If your Classic score's percentile already matches your targets, the retake question is about edition preference, not performance.

What actually went wrong the first time?

A retake without a diagnosis is a coin flip. Before booking anything, sort your first attempt into one of three causes, because each demands a different fix and a different timeline.

  • Content gaps. Misses spread across many skills, and accuracy stays low even without a timer. Your official score report labels the skills involved; when several are weak in the same section, the section score follows. The fix is a rebuild, usually 8 to 12 weeks, working skill by skill and returning to misses on a schedule. This is the case our trainer is built for: it keeps a rating per skill keyed to the official score report skill labels and brings missed questions back on a spaced schedule.
  • Pacing. Accuracy is fine untimed, but a section collapsed on the clock, often with a string of guesses at the end. Pacing is trainable: the Focus Edition gives you 45 minutes per section and lets you bookmark and change up to 3 answers per section (mba.com, GMAC, 2026), which rewards a checkpoint plan. Budget 4 to 8 weeks of timed sets, using the methods in our GMAT time management guide.
  • Nerves or test day conditions. Practice tests ran consistently higher than the official score, and you can point to poor sleep, anxiety, or a disrupted testing environment. The content is already there. This fix is the shortest: retake soon, after a few full-length rehearsals under strict conditions.

If you cannot tell which of the three happened, that is a finding too. Take one full-length practice test under real timing before deciding anything. The pattern of misses will usually name the cause.

How much time remains before your deadlines?

Count backward from the application round you actually intend to hit. A pacing or nerves fix needs roughly 4 to 8 weeks; a content rebuild needs 8 to 12. Then add scheduling time, since you need an open test appointment, a margin for scores to arrive, and room for the essays you are not writing while you study.

If that arithmetic leaves fewer than about four weeks of real preparation, a retake is usually the wrong move for this round. A rushed second attempt tends to reproduce the first score, and it spends exactly the weeks the rest of the application needed. Targeting the next round with a full preparation window usually beats a compromised score in this one.

What does GMAC's retake policy allow?

GMAC, the exam's maker, limits how many times you can sit the GMAT within a 12 month window and across a lifetime, and it requires a waiting period between attempts. The specific limits can change, so check the current policy on the official GMAT page at mba.com before you book anything.

The practical consequence is that attempts are a budget. Spend one only when the diagnosis and the calendar both support it, not as a reflex after a disappointing score report.

The bottom line

Retake the GMAT when the gap is real, the cause has a name, and the calendar allows a proper fix. Twenty or more Focus Edition points below your target schools' published numbers justifies the work. A clear diagnosis of content, pacing, or nerves tells you what the work is. Four to twelve weeks, depending on that diagnosis, is what the work takes. Check the current attempt rules on mba.com, book the date that fits the fix, and let every study hour answer the specific failure of the first attempt.
